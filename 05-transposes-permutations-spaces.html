Transposes, Permutations, Spaces Rn

The lecture first finishes elimination: row exchanges, PA = LU, transposes and symmetric matrices. Then it opens the real subject of the course: vector spaces and subspaces, the sets of vectors you can reach. For a robot, a subspace is the set of all motions it is able to make.

5.0

Start here: one job to finish, one idea to begin

What you already know, and the two questions this lecture answers.

This lecture has two halves. The first half finishes the story of elimination. The second half begins a new story that runs through the rest of the course.

What you already know. Lecture 2 solved Ax = b by elimination: subtract multiples of one row from the rows below it until the matrix has zeros under its diagonal. The number used to clear each column is a pivot, and a pivot is never allowed to be zero. Lecture 4 packed the whole process into one statement, A = LU.

The job to finish. Both lectures assumed that every pivot turned out non-zero. What happens when a zero sits where a pivot should be? The cure is to swap two rows, which is called a row exchange. This lecture gives the matrix that does the swapping, and two small tools that come along with it: the transpose and the symmetric matrix.

The idea to begin. Until now every question was about one system and one answer. Strang now steps back and looks at whole sets of vectors. Which sets can you add and stretch inside without ever falling out? Those sets are called vector spaces, and the smaller ones that live inside them are subspaces. They are the real subject of linear algebra.

Robotics linkwhy a robot cares

From "solve this one" to "what can I ever do?"

A robot's controller solves one system at a time: this motion, these motor commands. A robot's designer asks a larger question: what is the complete set of motions this machine can make, whatever the commands? That set turns out to be a subspace. A rover that cannot slide sideways owns a flat sheet inside the space of all motions. A robot on omni wheels owns the whole space.

The first half of this page gives a robot two everyday tools: elimination that cannot be tripped up by a badly placed number, and the transpose, which turns a force at the hand into torques at the motors. The second half teaches you to see the sets.

5.1

Row exchanges and PA = LU

What to do when a zero sits in the pivot position.

Elimination needs a non-zero number in each position along the diagonal. If it finds a zero there, it looks further down the same column for a non-zero entry and swaps that row up. The equations are the same equations in a new order, so the solution does not change.

A row exchange is done by a matrix. Start from the identity matrix I, which has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere and leaves every matrix unchanged. Re-order the rows of I and you get a permutation matrix P. Multiplying PA re-orders the rows of A in exactly the same way.

One exchange, as a matrix
=

How many permutation matrices are there?

For 3 rows there are 3 choices for the row that goes first, then 2 for the next, then 1. That is 3 · 2 · 1 = 6. In general the count is n · (n − 1) · … · 1, written n! and read "n factorial". There are 6 permutation matrices of size 3 and 24 of size 4.

All six permutation matrices of size 3

The first is the identity, which exchanges nothing. The next three swap one pair of rows. The last two move all three rows round in a cycle. Multiply any two of them and you get another one of the six.

Undoing a permutation costs nothing

Flip a matrix over its diagonal, so that its rows become its columns, and you have its transpose, written with a small T (section 5.3 looks at it properly). For a permutation matrix the transpose is the inverse:

P−1 = PT  which is the same as  PTP = I
=

The reason is short. Each row of P holds a single 1. A row multiplied into itself gives 1 · 1 = 1. Two different rows have their ones in different places, so they multiply to 0. Those products are exactly the entries of PTP.

The factorization that always works

Lecture 4's A = LU breaks down when a pivot position holds a zero. The repair is to put the rows in a good order first and then factor:

The rule from the lecture
PA = LU

P holds the row exchanges, L holds the multipliers and U is the upper triangular result. This works for every invertible matrix A, meaning every matrix whose system has exactly one solution. When no exchange is needed, P = I and you are back at A = LU.

A worked example of our own (the lecture states the rule without numbers)

The matrix A above has 0 in its first pivot position. Exchange rows 1 and 2. Then eliminate as usual: row 2 already has a 0 in the first column, subtract 2 × row 1 from row 3, then subtract 3 × row 2 from row 3. The multipliers 0, 2 and 3 go into L.

=

Check the bottom row of LU: 2 · (1, 2, 1) + 3 · (0, 1, 1) + (0, 0, 4) = (2, 7, 9), which is row 3 of A.

Robotics linkordering and labelling

A permutation is a re-labelling, and robots re-label all the time

Each row of a robot's system comes from somewhere: one sensor reading, or one balance of forces. Exchanging rows means choosing which sensor's equation the solver deals with first. Nothing about the robot changes. Only the order of the paperwork does.

The same matrix describes wiring. Suppose three wheel encoders are plugged into the wrong ports, so the computer receives the readings in the order (2, 3, 1). That scrambling is a permutation matrix P applied to the true readings. To put them right the software multiplies by PT. It never has to compute an inverse, because for a permutation the transpose is the inverse.

Key idea

A permutation changes the order of the equations and nothing else. With the right order in hand, every invertible matrix factors as PA = LU.

5.2

Why software pivots even when it does not have to

A pivot that is tiny is almost as dangerous as a pivot that is zero.

On paper a row exchange is needed only for a zero pivot. Strang points out that software such as Matlab exchanges rows far more often than that. It does so whenever the pivot is small compared with the entries below it.

The reason is that a computer does not keep every digit. It stores a fixed number of significant digits, which are the digits of a number counted from its first non-zero digit. So 0.000123 and 123000 both have three. After every multiplication and subtraction the result is rounded to fit.

Now think about the multiplier, the number that elimination multiplies the pivot row by before subtracting it. The multiplier is an entry divided by the pivot. Divide by a tiny pivot and the multiplier is huge. The row being changed is then swamped by a huge multiple of the pivot row, and its own numbers are pushed past the last digit the computer keeps.

The cure is a habit called partial pivoting: before clearing a column, look down it, find the entry that is largest in size, and exchange that row into the pivot position. Every multiplier is then 1 or smaller in size, and nothing gets swamped.

A small pivot, worked with three significant digits
0.0001x + y = 1  and  x + y = 2

The true answer is x = 1.0001, y = 0.9999, to four decimals. Now pretend the computer keeps only three significant digits after every step.

Without an exchange the pivot is 0.0001 and the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.0001 = 10000. Row 2 becomes 1 − 10000 = −9999, which three digits store as −10000. Its right side becomes 2 − 10000 = −9998, also stored as −10000. So y = 1, and then x = (1 − 1) ÷ 0.0001 = 0. The computed answer is (0, 1). The value of x is completely wrong.

With the rows exchanged the pivot is 1 and the multiplier is 0.0001. Row 2 becomes 1 − 0.0001 = 0.9999, stored as 1, with right side 1 − 0.0002 = 0.9998, stored as 1. So y = 1 and x = (2 − 1) ÷ 1 = 1. The computed answer is (1, 1), which is right to three digits.

Look at what the rounding threw away in the first run. The 1 and the 2 were row 2's own numbers. They were everything the equation x + y = 2 had to say. Next to 10000 they sat in the fifth digit, and the computer kept three. After the rounding, row 2 reads −10000y = −10000, which is only a copy of row 1. The second equation had been wiped out.

Robotics linknumerical care

Do not divide by your weakest measurement

Read the two equations as two sensors measuring the same pair of unknowns. In the first equation x has the coefficient 0.0001: that sensor barely responds to x. The second sensor responds to x at full strength. Using the first row as the pivot row means working out x from the sensor that can hardly see it, by dividing a small leftover by 0.0001. Any rounding in that leftover is multiplied by ten thousand.

Partial pivoting is the software version of common sense: to pin down a quantity, use the equation that sees it best. A robot's controller solves systems like this a thousand times a second with numbers that come from real sensors, so its solver always pivots.

Watch out

This failure is different from the nearly parallel lines of Lecture 1. Here the two lines cross at a healthy 45° and the problem itself is easy. Only the order of the rows was bad, and an exchange cures it completely. When the lines really are nearly parallel, no exchange can help, because the trouble is in the problem and not in the method.

5.3

Transposes: rows become columns

One flip over the diagonal.

To transpose a matrix, write its first row as the first column, its second row as the second column, and so on. The result is written AT and read "A transpose".

The lecture's example
has the transpose

The red column (1, 2, 4) has become the red row. The blue column (3, 3, 1) has become the blue row. A matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns turns into one with 2 rows and 3 columns.

In symbols the rule is short. The entry in row i, column j of the transpose is the entry in row j, column i of the original:

(AT)ij = Aji

For example the 4 sits in row 3, column 1 of A and in row 1, column 3 of AT.

Picture the main diagonal, the line of entries running from the top left corner down to the right. Transposing is a flip across that line. Entries on the diagonal stay where they are. Every other entry trades places with its mirror image. Flip twice and you are back where you started: (AT)T = A.

One rule about products will be needed in the next section. The transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in reverse order: (AB)T = BTAT. The order reverses for the same reason it does for inverses. If you put on socks and then shoes, undoing it means shoes first.

Robotics linkframes and forces

A robot transposes whenever it changes point of view

A robot keeps a rotation matrix R that turns directions measured in its own body frame into directions in the world frame. To go the other way, from world to body, it needs the inverse of R. For a rotation matrix, as for a permutation matrix, the inverse is the transpose. So the robot never solves anything. It flips the matrix.

The second use is deeper and gets its own section. Motion flows from the joints out to the hand through a matrix J. Force flows from the hand back to the joints through JT. Section 5.5 shows why.

5.4

Symmetric matrices and RTR

Matrices that do not change when you flip them.

A matrix is symmetric when transposing leaves it unchanged: AT = A. Such a matrix must be square, and the entries on either side of the diagonal mirror each other.

The lecture's symmetric matrix

The two 1s mirror each other, the two 7s mirror each other and the two 9s mirror each other. The diagonal entries 3, 2 and 4 can be anything.

A factory for symmetric matrices

Take any matrix R at all. It can even be rectangular. Multiply its transpose by it. The product RTR is always symmetric.

The proof is one line, using the reverse-order rule from section 5.3. Transpose the product and see what comes back:

(RTR)T = RT(RT)T = RTR

Transposing gave back the same matrix, and that is the definition of symmetric.

Lab 5.2 showed the plain reason behind the proof. The entry in row 1, column 2 of the product is one row of numbers multiplied into another: 1 · 2 + 3 · 3 = 11. The entry in row 2, column 1 uses the same two lists the other way round: 2 · 1 + 3 · 3 = 11. Multiplying two lists entry by entry and adding is a dot product, and a dot product does not care which list comes first.

Robotics linkwhere they turn up

Symmetric matrices are everywhere in a robot

Whenever robot software multiplies a matrix by its own transpose, the result is symmetric. An arm's Jacobian J is the matrix from Lecture 1 whose columns say how each joint moves the hand. Both JTJ and JJT appear in arm control, and both are symmetric for every pose. Here is JJT for the arm pose used in the next section:

=

The two mirrored entries are both −0.2. Two more symmetric matrices sit at the heart of every robot:

  • The inertia matrix (or mass matrix) says how hard each joint is to speed up. Entry (1, 2) is how much joint 2's motion loads joint 1, and that coupling is the same in both directions.
  • The covariance matrix of sensor noise says how the errors of two sensors rise and fall together. The link between sensor 1 and sensor 2 is the same as the link between sensor 2 and sensor 1.

A practical use: if a program prints one of these matrices and two mirrored entries differ, there is a bug.

5.5

The robot: forces travel through the transpose

The lecture's small tool, doing a large job.

Lecture 1 met the two-link arm. Turn the shoulder at speed q̇1 and the elbow at speed q̇2, and the hand moves with a velocity that is a mix of the two columns of the Jacobian: v = Jq̇. That was a question about motion.

Here is a question about force. The hand has to push on something: hold up a load, or press a tool against a surface. Call the push F. What twist must each motor supply to hold it? The twist of a motor is its torque, written τ and measured in newton metres. A positive torque turns its joint counter-clockwise.

The argument, from energy

Power is energy delivered per second. For a push it is force times speed in the direction of the push. For a motor it is torque times turning speed. The arm has no engine in the hand, so whatever power the hand delivers has to come from the motors:

Power in equals power out, for every possible motion
τ1q̇1 + τ2q̇2=F · v=F · (q̇1 c1 + q̇2 c2)=q̇1(c1 · F) + q̇2(c2 · F)

Here c1 and c2 are the two columns of J. The two ends must agree whatever the joint speeds are, so the amounts multiplying q̇1 must match, and so must the amounts multiplying q̇2:

τ1 = c1 · F  and  τ2 = c2 · F  which together say  τ = JTF

Each torque is one column of J dotted with the force. Lay those columns down as rows and you have JT.

Worked numbers

The arm has an upper link of 0.5 m and a forearm of 0.4 m. The upper link points straight up and the forearm is level, like a waiter's arm under a tray. The elbow is at (0, 0.5) and the hand at (0.4, 0.5). The hand pushes straight up with 10 N, enough to carry about one kilogram.

  = =

Shoulder: (−0.5)(0) + (0.4)(10) = 4. Elbow: (0)(0) + (0.4)(10) = 4. Each motor must twist counter-clockwise with 4 N·m.

What the numbers mean: force times lever arm

Column i of J is the arrow from joint i to the hand, turned a quarter turn. Dotting it with the force gives the force multiplied by its lever arm: the distance from the joint to the line along which the force acts. That is the turning effect a wrench has on a bolt. In the example both joints are 0.4 m to the side of the upward push, so both carry 10 × 0.4 = 4 N·m.

Two consequences follow, and you can see both in the lab.

  • A force aimed through a joint costs that joint nothing. Its lever arm there is zero.
  • A force along a straight arm costs both joints nothing. The line of the force runs through the elbow and the shoulder, so the links carry the load and the motors rest. A weightlifter does this by locking the arms out overhead.
Robotics linkforce and motion duality

One matrix, two jobs

Velocity goes outward through J: joint speeds in, hand velocity out. Force comes inward through JT: hand force in, joint torques out. Roboticists call the pair dual. The same four numbers serve both, read by columns for motion and by rows for force. This is how a robot arm pushes with a chosen force when it has no force sensor in the hand: the controller computes JTF and commands those torques.

The duality also explains the straight arm. Lecture 1 found that a straight arm cannot move its hand along its own length. Now you can see the other face of that fact: along that same direction it can resist any force for free. The direction an arm cannot move in is the direction it holds without effort.

The model here is the simplest honest one: a flat arm, held still, with no friction and no weight in the links. A real controller adds the torque that holds up the arm itself.

Velocity uses the columns of J. Force uses the same numbers read as rows. That is the whole meaning of the transpose in robotics.

5.6

Vector spaces: R2, R3, Rn

The second half of the lecture begins here.

There are two things you can do with vectors: add two of them, and multiply one by a number. A vector space is a set of vectors in which you can do both, as much as you like, and never leave the set.

Said the other way round: take any vectors from the set, stretch each by any number and add the results. That mix is a linear combination, the central operation from Lecture 1. In a vector space every linear combination of its members is again a member. Mathematicians say the set is closed under adding and scaling.

The most important vector spaces have short names:

  • R2 is the set of all vectors with two real components, such as (3, 2), (0, 0) or (π, e). You can picture it as the whole x-y plane.
  • R3 is the set of all vectors with three real components: all of three-dimensional space.
  • Rn is the set of all vectors with n real components. Nobody can draw it beyond n = 3, but the two operations work in exactly the same way.

The zero vector is in every vector space. The zero vector 0 has every component equal to 0. It has to be a member, because you are allowed to multiply any member by the number 0.

The full textbook definition also lists eight rules of arithmetic, such as v + w = w + v. For ordinary vectors of numbers those rules hold automatically, so the working test is the one above: add, scale, and check you are still inside.

The lecture's example of a set that fails

Take only the first quadrant of the plane: every vector whose two components are both zero or positive. Add two such vectors and both components are still zero or positive, so adding is safe. Now multiply by a negative number:

(−1)=  which has left the quadrant

One failure is enough. The first quadrant is not a vector space.

Robotics linkthe space of motions

Every motion of a robot on a floor is one vector in R3

A robot driving on a flat floor is doing three things at any instant: moving forward at some speed vx, sliding sideways at some speed vy, and turning at some rate ω. Stack them and the motion is a single vector (vx, vy, ω), called the body velocity.

Two motions can be added: drive forward while turning. A motion can be scaled: the same thing faster, slower, or backwards. So the set of all body velocities is the vector space R3. A robot on omni wheels can produce every vector in it and so owns the whole space.

The zero vector has a plain meaning too. "Every vector space contains the zero vector" says that standing still is always one of the robot's options.

5.7

Subspaces and the two tests

A vector space living inside a bigger one.

A subspace is a part of a vector space that is a vector space in its own right. To check a candidate set, run two tests:

  • Adding. For any v and w in the set, is v + w in the set?
  • Scaling. For any v in the set and any number c, is cv in the set?

Both must pass for every choice. A quick first check saves time: is the zero vector in the set? If it is missing, stop. The set cannot be a subspace.

The lecture's two lines

A line through the origin is a subspace of R2. Every vector on it is a multiple of one direction. Add two multiples of a direction and you get a multiple of it. Scale one and you get another.

A line that misses the origin is not. The zero vector is not on it. Scaling fails as well: doubling a vector on the line carries it off the line.

Robotics linklimits are not subspaces

The sets that fail are the limits of real hardware

Each failing set in the lab is something a real robot lives with.

  • One-way thrusters. A thruster can push but cannot pull, so its thrust is zero or positive. Two of them reach a set shaped like the first quadrant. The scaling test fails for negative numbers, which is the mathematics of "there is no reverse".
  • A speed limit. Every velocity up to top speed is a disc. Scaling fails: twice an allowed velocity may be too fast.
  • Joint limits. Each joint speed is allowed a range, so the allowed set is a box. It fails for the same reason as the disc.
  • A fixed, non-zero target. The set of all motor speeds that produce one particular motion, such as exactly 1 m/s forward, is a line or plane that misses the origin. Standing still is not in it.

This is why the subject is organised the way it is. Linear algebra gives clean, complete answers for the subspace, the set of motions the mechanism allows. A real controller then lays the limits on top as a second step.

Watch out

Containing the zero vector is necessary but not enough. The first quadrant and the disc both contain it, and neither is a subspace. Use the zero test to throw sets out quickly, and the two tests to let sets in.

5.8

Every subspace of R2 and R3

The complete list is short.

The two tests are strict, and very few shapes pass them. Strang lists every subspace there is.

All the subspaces of R²
  1. The whole plane R2 itself.
  2. Any line through the origin (0, 0). There are infinitely many, one for each direction.
  3. The zero vector alone. Strang calls this smallest subspace Z.
All the subspaces of R³
  1. All of R3.
  2. Any plane through the origin.
  3. Any line through the origin.
  4. The zero vector alone.

Notice what is missing. A plane that does not pass through the origin, a curved surface, a half-line, a box, a ball: none of them is a subspace. Every subspace is flat, runs on for ever in every direction it contains, and passes through the origin.

A line through the origin inside R3 is a subspace of R3, but it is not the same thing as R1. Its vectors still have three components.

Robotics linkfour kinds of machine

The list of subspaces is a catalogue of mobile robots

Take the space of body velocities (vx, vy, ω) and ask what set of motions a wheeled machine can produce. If its motion is a matrix times its wheel speeds, the answer is always one of the four shapes on Strang's list:

  • The zero vector alone: a parked machine with its brakes on.
  • A line through the origin: a cart on a straight rail. It can go forward and backward at any speed and do nothing else.
  • A plane through the origin: a two-wheel rover. It can drive and turn in any mix, but it can never slide sideways.
  • All of R3: a robot on omni wheels, which can do everything.

So a designer can describe what a mechanism is able to do with one word: a point, a line, a plane or the whole space.

5.9

The column space: a subspace built from a matrix

Where the lecture ends, and where the next one starts.

Where do subspaces come from in practice? Strang closes the lecture with the most important source: the columns of a matrix.

The lecture's example
A=

The two columns (1, 2, 4) and (3, 3, 1) are vectors in R3. On their own, two vectors are not a subspace. So take them and all their linear combinations: every vector of the form x1(1, 2, 4) + x2(3, 3, 1).

That set is a subspace. Add two combinations of the columns and you get a combination of the columns. Scale a combination and you get a combination. It is called the column space of A, written C(A).

What does it look like? The two columns do not lie along the same line, so their combinations spread out into a flat sheet: a plane through the origin of R3. With a little arithmetic you can name the plane. It is 10x − 11y + 3z = 0, and both columns satisfy it: 10 − 22 + 12 = 0 and 30 − 33 + 3 = 0.

This ties back to the question that closed Lecture 1: can Ax = b be solved? The left side Ax is a combination of the columns, so the system can be solved exactly when b lies in the column space. For this matrix, most right-hand sides in R3 are off the plane and have no solution. Lecture 6 starts from here.

Robotics linkwhat the wheels can do

A rover's possible motions are the column space of its wheel matrix

A differential-drive rover has two powered wheels side by side, a distance W apart (the track width), each of radius r. The left wheel turns at ωL and the right at ωR, so their rims roll over the ground at rωL and rωR.

  • The body moves forward at the average of the two: vx = r(ωL + ωR)/2.
  • It turns when the right side outruns the left: ω = r(ωR − ωL)/W.
  • Wheels roll forward and do not slide along their axle, so the sideways speed is vy = 0.

With r = 0.1 m and W = 0.5 m those three lines are one matrix equation:

Wheel speeds of 8 and 12 rad/s give (1.0, 0, 0.8): one metre per second forward while turning left. Every body velocity the rover can produce is a combination of the two columns, so the set of achievable velocities is C(A). The middle row of A is all zeros, so that column space is the plane vy = 0: a subspace of R3, and a picture of the fact that the rover cannot slide sideways. The model assumes ideal wheels that never slip.

P1Write the permutation matrix P that re-orders the rows of a 3-row matrix into the order (row 3, row 1, row 2). Find its inverse. How many permutation matrices of size 5 are there?permutations

Take the identity and put its rows in the order 3, 1, 2. The inverse is the transpose: flip P over its diagonal.

Check on a column of labels: P sends (1, 2, 3) to (3, 1, 2), and PT sends (3, 1, 2) back to (1, 2, 3).

Answer: the two matrices above, and 5! = 5 · 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 120 permutation matrices of size 5.

P2Factor as PA = LU.PA = LU

The first pivot is 1. Subtract 2 × row 1 from row 2 to get (0, 0, 3), and 4 × row 1 from row 3 to get (0, 2, 4).

The second pivot position now holds 0, and row 3 has a 2 below it. Exchange rows 2 and 3. The matrix is then upper triangular with pivots 1, 2, 3, so the last multiplier is 0.

The multipliers travel with their rows: the row that ends up second was built with multiplier 4, and the row that ends up third with multiplier 2.

=

Answer: as shown. Check the last row of LU: 2 · (1, 1, 1) + (0, 0, 3) = (2, 2, 5), which is row 2 of A, now sitting third.

Follow-up: change the 5 to a 2. Then row 2 is twice row 1, the last pivot is 0, and no exchange helps. The matrix is singular.

P4Which of these sets are subspaces? (a) all (x, y) with y = 3x; (b) all (x, y) with y = 3x + 1; (c) all (x, y) with xy = 0, which is the two axes together; (d) all (x, y, z) with x + y + z = 0; (e) all (x, y, z) with x + y + z = 1.subspaces
  1. Yes. A line through the origin.
  2. No. (0, 0) gives 0 = 1, so the zero vector is missing.
  3. No. It contains the zero vector and passes the scaling test, but (1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1), and 1 · 1 ≠ 0. Adding fails.
  4. Yes. A plane through the origin. If two vectors each have components adding to 0, so do their sum and any multiple.
  5. No. The zero vector is missing. Also (1, 0, 0) + (0, 1, 0) = (1, 1, 0), whose components add to 2.

Answer: (a) and (d) only. Compare (c) with the first quadrant: the quadrant passes adding and fails scaling, and the two axes do the opposite. Both tests are needed.

P5Let = A. Describe its column space. Is (2, 5, 7) in it? Is (1, 1, 1)?column space

The columns (1, 2, 3) and (0, 1, 1) do not lie on one line, so C(A) is a plane through the origin. In both columns the third component is the sum of the first two, and that stays true for every combination. So the plane is z = x + y.

(2, 5, 7): 2 + 5 = 7, so it is on the plane. The first component needs 2 of column 1, and then 5 = 2 · 2 + 1 needs 1 of column 2.

(1, 1, 1): 1 + 1 ≠ 1, so it is off the plane.

Answer: C(A) is the plane z = x + y. (2, 5, 7) = 2 × column 1 + 1 × column 2 is in it. (1, 1, 1) is not, so Ax = (1, 1, 1) has no solution.

Follow-up: replace the second column by (2, 4, 6). It is now twice the first, and the column space shrinks to a line.

P6A computer keeps only two significant digits. Solve 0.001x + y = 1, x + y = 2 on it, first with no row exchange and then with the rows exchanged. The true answer is close to (1, 1).pivoting

No exchange. Multiplier 1 ÷ 0.001 = 1000. Row 2: 1 − 1000 = −999, kept as −1000. Right side: 2 − 1000 = −998, kept as −1000. So y = 1 and x = (1 − 1) ÷ 0.001 = 0.

Rows exchanged. Multiplier 0.001. Row 2: 1 − 0.001 = 0.999, kept as 1. Right side: 1 − 0.002 = 0.998, kept as 1. So y = 1 and x = (2 − 1) ÷ 1 = 1.

Answer: (0, 1) without the exchange and (1, 1) with it. The true answer is (1.001, 0.999) to three decimals, so only the second is acceptable.

P8An arm has two links of length 1 m. The first link lies along the x axis and the elbow is bent 90°, so the elbow is at (1, 0) and the hand at (1, 1). Its Jacobian is . Find the joint torques when the hand pushes straight down with 6 N, and when it pushes along +x with 4 N.robot

Use τ = JTF. The transpose is .

Pushing down, F = (0, −6): τ1 = (−1)(0) + (1)(−6) = −6 and τ2 = (−1)(0) + (0)(−6) = 0.

Pushing along +x, F = (4, 0): τ1 = −4 and τ2 = −4.

Answer: (−6, 0) N·m, then (−4, −4) N·m. In the first case the elbow motor does nothing. The forearm is vertical and so is the force, so the push runs straight through the elbow and has no lever arm there. The shoulder is 1 m to the side of it and carries 6 × 1 = 6 N·m, clockwise.

Follow-up: straighten the arm along the x axis. The Jacobian becomes [0 0; 2 1], and a push of any size along +x gives torques (0, 0).

P10A small boat has two one-way thrusters, each with thrust zero or positive. One pushes the boat along (1, 0) and the other along (0, 1). (a) Which forces can it produce, and is that set a subspace? (b) A third one-way thruster is added, pushing along (−1, −1). Which forces can it produce now? Find thrusts that give the force (−2, 1).robot

(a) The force is t1(1, 0) + t2(0, 1) = (t1, t2) with both thrusts zero or positive. That is the first quadrant. It is not a subspace: (1, 1) is in it and (−1)(1, 1) is not. The boat cannot push backwards.

(b) Fire the third thruster with thrust t3. It contributes (−t3, −t3), which can make both components as negative as you like. The first two thrusters then add back whatever is needed. For (−2, 1): take t3 = 2 to get (−2, −2), then t2 = 3 to lift the second component to 1.

Answer: (a) the first quadrant, not a subspace. (b) Every force in the plane, which is the subspace R2. Thrusts (0, 3, 2) give (−2, 1).

The design lesson: actuators that work in one direction only need one more than the number of directions to be covered. Three one-way thrusters do the job that two reversible ones would do.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each one.

Q1What is a permutation matrix, how many are there of size 4, and what is the inverse of one?

It is the identity matrix with its rows re-ordered. There are 4! = 24 of size 4. The inverse is the transpose, P−1 = PT.

Q2Why is the factorization written PA = LU instead of A = LU, and for which matrices does it work?

A = LU fails when a zero turns up in a pivot position. P re-orders the rows so that every pivot is non-zero, and then ordinary elimination gives L and U. It works for every invertible matrix.

Q3The pivot is 0.0001, which is not zero. Why does software exchange rows anyway?

Dividing by a tiny pivot gives a huge multiplier. The row being changed is swamped by a huge multiple of the pivot row, and its own numbers are rounded away. Choosing the largest entry in the column as the pivot (partial pivoting) keeps every multiplier at 1 or below in size. In the worked example three-digit arithmetic gave x = 0 without the exchange and x = 1 with it.

Q4What is the transpose of , and why is a matrix times its own transpose always symmetric?

The transpose is : rows become columns. Transposing RTR gives RT(RT)T = RTR, the same matrix again. In plain terms, entries (i, j) and (j, i) are the same two lists of numbers dotted together in opposite orders.

Q5An arm is stretched out straight and the hand pushes along the line of the arm. What torques do the motors need, and why?

None. Each torque is the force times its lever arm about that joint, and the line of the force passes through both joints, so both lever arms are zero. In matrix terms, both columns of J are at right angles to the arm, so JTF = 0. The links carry the load.

Q6Is the first quadrant of the plane a subspace? Which test does it fail?

No. Adding two of its vectors stays inside, but multiplying by a negative number leaves. A set of one-way thrusters behaves the same way: more push is available, the opposite push is not.

Q7List every kind of subspace of three-dimensional space, and name a wheeled machine for each.

The zero vector alone (a parked machine), a line through the origin (a cart on a rail), a plane through the origin (a two-wheel rover, which cannot slide sideways), and all of R3 (a robot on omni wheels).
