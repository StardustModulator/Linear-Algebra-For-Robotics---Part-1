Elimination with Matrices

Elimination is the method every computer uses to solve Ax = b. This lecture does it by hand once, on three equations, and then shows that each step of the method is itself a matrix. The robot of the lecture has three omni wheels, and it uses elimination to turn three encoder readings into one body velocity.

2.0

Start here: why we need a method

Lecture 1 showed what a system of equations means. This lecture shows how to solve one.

In Lecture 1 a whole system of linear equations became one short sentence, Ax = b. We learned to see its solution as lines that cross, or as arrows that add up. What we never did was find that solution by a method that always works.

With two equations you can draw the two lines and read off the crossing point. With three you would need planes in space. With a hundred there is no picture at all. So we want an algorithm: a fixed list of steps that a person or a computer can follow without having to be clever.

The algorithm is called elimination, or Gaussian elimination after the mathematician Gauss. Strang opens the lecture by saying that this is the way every software package solves equations. He works one example all the way through. It has three equations and three unknowns:

The lecture's system
x + 2y + z = 2  3x + 8y + z = 12  4y + z = 2
=

The third equation contains no x, so the first number in its row is 0.

The lecture has four parts, and so does this page:

  1. Eliminate. Subtract equations from one another until the system has a staircase shape.
  2. Back-substitute. Solve the staircase from the bottom step upward.
  3. Know when it fails. See how the method breaks down, and what a breakdown tells you.
  4. Write every step as a matrix. This is the new idea, and the reason for the lecture's title.
Robotics linkwhy a robot cares

A robot needs a method that never has to think

A robot's computer solves systems like this one hundreds of times every second: wheel readings go in, body motion comes out. It cannot look at a picture and it cannot guess. Elimination suits it for three reasons. The steps are the same every time. The number of steps is known in advance, so the answer always arrives on schedule. And when no answer exists, the method stops at one clear, recognisable point, which the robot can treat as an alarm.

2.1

Elimination: pivots and multipliers

Use one equation to remove an unknown from the equations below it.

Elimination rests on one permitted move: subtract a multiple of one equation from another. If two facts are true, then the second fact minus three times the first is true as well. Nothing is lost either, because adding the same multiple back would restore the equation you started with.

The purpose of each move is to make one unknown disappear. Look at the first two equations. Equation 1 begins with 1x and equation 2 begins with 3x. Subtract 3 times equation 1 from equation 2 and the x terms cancel. Equation 2 is left with only y and z.

The letters x, y, z do no work in this. Only the coefficients change. So we run the whole method on the matrix A and leave the letters out. Three words are needed first:

  • The diagonal of a matrix runs from its top-left corner to its bottom-right corner.
  • A pivot is the entry on the diagonal that we use to clear the entries beneath it. The first pivot is the top-left entry.
  • A multiplier says how many times the pivot's row must be subtracted. It is always the entry to remove, divided by the pivot.

  1. Step (2,1). The first pivot is 1. The entry below it is 3, so the multiplier is 3 ÷ 1 = 3. Row 2 − 3 × row 1 is [3 8 1] − [3 6 3] = [0 2 −2].
  2. Step (3,1). The entry in row 3, column 1 is already 0. The multiplier is 0 and there is nothing to do.
  3. Step (3,2). The second pivot is 2. The entry below it is 4, so the multiplier is 4 ÷ 2 = 2. Row 3 − 2 × row 2 is [0 4 1] − [0 4 −4] = [0 0 5].
  4. The third pivot is 5. Nothing lies below it, so elimination is finished. The pivots are 1, 2 and 5.

The finished matrix is named U, because it is upper triangular: every entry below the diagonal is 0. Getting from A to U is the whole purpose of elimination.

Strang labels each step by the position it clears. Step (2,1) clears row 2, column 1. Keep that labelling in mind. In section 2.4 the same numbers will name the matrices that perform the steps.

One remark in passing. Multiply the pivots together and you get 1 × 2 × 5 = 10. That number is the determinant of A. The course reaches determinants much later, so for now it is enough to notice that the pivots carry real information about the matrix.

Robotics linkwheel encoders

A pivot says how firmly an equation pins down an unknown

Take the simplest driving robot: two wheels side by side, a distance W apart. It has two unknowns, its forward speed v and its turn rate ω. Each wheel carries an encoder, a sensor that reports how fast the rim of the wheel is moving. The left wheel reads v − (W/2)ω and the right wheel reads v + (W/2)ω. Two readings, two equations:

= sleftsright

The first pivot is 1 and the multiplier is 1. Row 2 − row 1 leaves Wω = sright − sleft. Every two-wheeled robot performs this subtraction all day long: the difference of its wheel speeds, divided by W, is its turn rate.

The second pivot is W, the distance between the wheels. A wide robot has a large pivot, and its turn rate is firmly pinned down. A narrow robot has a small pivot. It has to divide by a small number, so a small encoder error becomes a large error in turn rate. That is a useful way to read any pivot: it measures how strongly its equation grips its unknown.

2.2

Back-substitution and the augmented matrix

Carry the right-hand side along, then solve from the bottom up.

So far only the left sides of the equations have changed. The right-hand sides must go through exactly the same subtractions, or the equations stop being true.

The tidy way to guarantee that is to write b as one extra column beside A. The result is the augmented matrix, written [A | b]. Augmented simply means that something has been added on. Every row operation now acts on a whole row, right-hand side included.

The lecture's numbers, with b carried along
→ →

In the last column, row 2 becomes 12 − 3 × 2 = 6 and row 3 becomes 2 − 2 × 6 = −10. The vector b = (2, 12, 2) has turned into c = (2, 6, −10). Now read the three rows as equations again:

x + 2y + z = 2  2y − 2z = 6  5z = −10

This new system, Ux = c, has the same solution as the original one, and it is easy to solve as long as you begin at the bottom. Solving upward like this is called back-substitution.

  1. Row 3 has one unknown. 5z = −10, so z = −2.
  2. Row 2 has two unknowns, but z is now known. 2y − 2(−2) = 6 gives 2y = 2, so y = 1.
  3. Row 1 has three unknowns, and two are known. x + 2(1) + (−2) = 2, so x = 2.

The solution is (x, y, z) = (2, 1, −2). Check it in the original equations: 2 + 2 − 2 = 2, 6 + 8 − 2 = 12 and 4 − 2 = 2. The last five steps of Lab 2.1 play this out.

Notice that every one of those steps divides by a pivot: by 5, then by 2, then by 1. That is a second reason why a pivot must never be zero.

Robotics linkorder of work

Known quantities first

Back-substitution is a habit every robot programmer has. Start with the equation that holds a single unknown. Then feed each answer into the equation above it. Robot software is arranged like this wherever it can be: if one measurement gives one quantity directly, that quantity is worked out first and then used to unlock the others.

You will see it happen in section 2.8. After elimination, the last row of the wheel equations gives the robot's turn rate on its own. Only then do the rows above it give the two sliding speeds.

2.3

When elimination fails

A zero in the pivot position. Sometimes a detour, sometimes the end of the road.

A pivot can never be zero. The multiplier is the entry to remove divided by the pivot, and nobody can divide by zero. Strang separates two kinds of trouble.

Temporary failure: exchange two rows

Suppose a 0 turns up in the pivot position, but somewhere below it in the same column there is an entry that is not 0. The cure is simple. Swap the two rows. This is a row exchange. Equations may be listed in any order, so swapping two of them changes nothing about the solution.

A zero that a row exchange cures

Take the lecture's matrix and change the 8 to a 6.

→ →

After step (2,1) the second pivot position holds 0. Below it sits a 4. Exchange rows 2 and 3 and the matrix is already upper triangular, with pivots 1, 4 and −2.

Permanent failure: nothing left to exchange

Now suppose the pivot position holds 0 and every entry below it is 0 too. No exchange can help. Elimination has run out of pivots.

The lecture's example of permanent failure

Strang changes the bottom-right entry of A from 1 to −4.

→ →

Row 3 − 2 × row 2 is [0 4 −4] − [0 4 −4] = [0 0 0]. The third pivot position holds 0 and no row lies below it. There are two pivots where three are needed.

A square matrix that cannot supply a full set of pivots is singular, the word you met in Lecture 1. Another name for it is not invertible. A matrix that does supply a full set of pivots is invertible, and its system has exactly one solution for every right-hand side.

What does the singular system actually say? With the lecture's b, the last row ends up as 0 = −10. No values of x, y, z can make that true, so there is no solution. With a different b the last row could read 0 = 0, which is true for every z, and then there are infinitely many solutions. These are the two singular cases of Lecture 1, the parallel lines and the repeated line, found this time by arithmetic.

Both failures are waiting in Lab 2.1. Load its second and third systems and step through them.

Robotics linkwhat a sensor can see

A zero pivot is a sensor with nothing to say

In a robot, each row is one sensor's equation and each column belongs to one unknown. A zero in the pivot position means that the sensor whose turn it is carries no information about the unknown it is being asked for.

Temporary. Often another sensor does know. Section 2.8 has a three-wheeled robot whose first wheel rolls exactly sideways, so its equation has a 0 in the forward-speed column. Elimination exchanges two rows and asks a different wheel first. Nothing is wrong with the robot.

Permanent. If all three wheels roll in the same direction, no wheel turns when the robot slides sideways. The sideways column of the matrix is all zeros and there is no row to exchange in. No software can repair that. Only re-mounting a wheel can.

Watch out

A pivot that is tiny, though not quite zero, is still trouble. Dividing by a small number magnifies every small error in the readings, so the answer exists but cannot be trusted. This is the ill-conditioning of Lecture 1 again. Real software therefore does not wait for an exact zero. At each step it exchanges rows to bring the largest available entry into the pivot position, a habit called partial pivoting.

2.4

Each step is a matrix

The idea that gives the lecture its title.

Until now elimination has been a list of spoken instructions: subtract this row from that one. Strang's main point is that each instruction can be written down as a matrix. Carrying out the step is then the same as multiplying by that matrix.

Two ways to read a product

First recall from Lecture 1 how a matrix multiplies a column. A matrix times a column is a combination of the columns of the matrix. The entries of the column say how much of each one to take.

Matrix times column: a combination of columns
= 2 + 1 − 2 =

This is the solution of section 2.2, read by columns: 2 of column 1, 1 of column 2 and −2 of column 3 add up to b.

Now put the single line of numbers on the other side. A list of numbers written sideways is a row vector. A row times a matrix is a combination of the rows of the matrix. The entries of the row say how much of each row to take.

Row times matrix: a combination of rows
= −3 + 1 + 0 =

The answer, [0 2 −2], is exactly the new row 2 from the first elimination step. So a row operation is a multiplication from the left.

The matrix for step (2,1)

We want a matrix that multiplies A on the left, leaves rows 1 and 3 alone, and replaces row 2 by row 2 − 3 × row 1. Build it one row at a time:

  • Row 1 of the answer should be row 1 of A. Take one of row 1 and none of the others: [1 0 0].
  • Row 2 of the answer should be −3 of row 1 plus one of row 2: [−3 1 0].
  • Row 3 of the answer should be row 3 of A: [0 0 1].

The matrix with rows [1 0 0], [0 1 0], [0 0 1] would leave every row alone. It is the identity matrix, written I. It does to a matrix what multiplying by 1 does to a number. Our matrix is the identity with one extra entry, a −3 in row 2, column 1. Strang calls it E21, an elimination matrix, numbered after the position it clears. Textbooks also say elementary matrix.

E21 subtracts 3 × row 1 from row 2. E32 subtracts 2 × row 2 from row 3. Together: E32 (E21 A) = U.

The recipe is always the same. Start from the identity and write minus the multiplier in the position you want to clear. Step (3,1) had multiplier 0, so its matrix E31 is the identity itself.

Robotics linksoftware

Elimination matrices are recorded instructions

A matrix such as E21 is one step of a procedure, stored as data. That matters to a robot. Its computer can keep the steps, replay them on new numbers and, as section 2.7 shows, run them backwards.

A robot's wheel geometry does not change while it drives, so the matrix A stays the same from one moment to the next. Only the readings change. The elimination steps for A can therefore be worked out once and replayed on every fresh set of readings.

2.5

Brackets may move, order may not

One law you may always use, and one you must never assume.

Elimination on the lecture's matrix is now a single line, E32 (E21 A) = U. The brackets say: apply E21 to A first, then apply E32 to the result.

Could we multiply the two E's together first, and get one matrix that does the whole job? Yes. Matrix multiplication obeys the associative law, which says you may move the brackets:

E32 (E21 A) = (E32 E21) A

So give the product a name, E = E32 E21. Then E A = U, and one matrix carries all of elimination.

The lecture's product, in the right order
=

Where does the 6 come from? Row 3 of E says how row 3 of U is built from the rows of A: 6 of row 1, −2 of row 2 and 1 of row 3. Step (3,2) subtracted twice the new row 2, and the new row 2 already contained −3 of row 1. Minus 2 times minus 3 is 6. Check it: 6[1 2 1] − 2[3 8 1] + [0 4 1] = [0 0 5].

What you may not do is change the order. Matrix multiplication is not commutative: for two matrices A and B, the products AB and BA are usually different.

The same two matrices, in the opposite order
=

The 6 has gone. In this order step (3,2) happens first and uses the old row 2, which has no row 1 mixed into it yet. Applied to A, this product does not give U.

Strang's summary of the two rules is the one to remember: you can move the parentheses, but you cannot move the matrices.

Robotics linkcontrol loop

Do the work once, and keep the order

Two things follow for a robot. First, because E is one stored matrix, the computer can apply it to each new vector of readings with a single multiplication. For the lecture's numbers, Eb = c: multiply E by (2, 12, 2) and you get (2, 6, −10), the same c that elimination produced row by row. Lecture 4 turns this into the standard way to solve quickly.

Second, robots feel non-commutativity in their own motion. Turn a quarter turn and then drive one metre, and you finish in a different place than if you drive one metre and then turn. Lecture 3 looks at that closely.

2.6

Permutation matrices

A row exchange is a matrix too.

The row exchange of section 2.3 can also be done by multiplying. The matrix that does it is a permutation matrix, written P. A permutation is a re-ordering.

Strang shows the 2 by 2 case. To exchange the two rows of a matrix, multiply it on the left by this P:

P on the left exchanges rows
=

Read P by rows. Its first row is [0 1]: take none of row 1 and one of row 2. So the new first row is [c d]. Its second row is [1 0], so the new second row is [a b].

A permutation matrix is easy to build. It is the identity matrix with its own rows re-ordered.

Now move the same P to the right-hand side of the matrix.

P on the right exchanges columns
=

This time read P by columns, because a matrix times a column is a combination of columns. The first column of P is (0, 1): none of column 1 and one of column 2. So the new first column is (b, d). The columns have changed places and the rows have stayed where they were.

Key idea

Row operations multiply on the left. Column operations multiply on the right. Elimination uses row operations only, which is why every E and every P stands to the left of A.

Robotics linkwiring and labels

Re-ordering sensors, re-ordering unknowns

Exchanging rows re-orders the equations. In a robot that means listing the sensors in a different order, as happens when two encoder cables are plugged into each other's sockets. The robot and its motion are unchanged. Only the order of the rows is different, and a P on the left describes it.

Exchanging columns re-orders the unknowns. That happens when two axes are re-labelled, or when two pieces of software number the motors differently. The entries of x must then be re-ordered to match, and a P on the right describes it. Mixing up the two is a classic source of bugs: one changes the list of sensors, the other changes the meaning of the answer.

2.7

Undoing a step: a first inverse

Every elimination step can be taken back.

E21 subtracted 3 × row 1 from row 2. How would you undo that? Add 3 × row 1 back to row 2. That instruction is a matrix as well: the identity with +3 in row 2, column 1.

The lecture's first inverse
=

Row 2 of the product is 3 × [1 0 0] + 1 × [−3 1 0] = [0 1 0]. The two steps cancel and the identity is left.

The matrix that undoes E21 is called its inverse, written E21−1. In general the inverse of a matrix M is the matrix M−1 for which M−1M = I. Apply M, then M−1, and you are back where you began.

For an elimination matrix the inverse costs nothing to find: flip the sign of the multiplier. For a row exchange it is easier still. Exchange the same two rows a second time, so that P is its own inverse.

Some matrices have no inverse. A singular matrix throws information away (remember the row of zeros in section 2.3), and nothing can bring back information that has been thrown away. Lecture 3 takes up inverses properly.

Robotics linkreplay and undo

A robot must be able to go back

Robots undo things all the time. An arm that has reached into a box retraces its moves to come out again. A mobile robot that knows the motion from its dock to a shelf needs the opposite motion to get home. Each motion is stored as a matrix, and going back means applying the inverse matrices in the opposite order, so the last step is undone first.

Elimination works the same way. To recover A from U, undo step (3,2) first and step (2,1) second. Lecture 4 builds a whole factorization out of that observation.

2.8

The robot: omni-wheel odometry

Three wheels, three encoder readings, one body velocity.

Everything in this lecture now goes to work on one machine.

An omni wheel has small free rollers set around its rim. It grips the floor along its rolling direction and slips freely sideways. A platform with three of them can drive in any direction and turn at the same time, which makes the design popular for warehouse carts and robot football.

Seen from above, the robot's motion at any instant is three numbers, together called its body velocity:

  • vx, how fast it moves straight ahead, in metres per second;
  • vy, how fast it slides to its left, in metres per second;
  • ω, how fast it turns, in radians per second, counter-clockwise counted as positive.

The robot cannot measure these three numbers directly. What it can measure is how fast each wheel is rolling. Each wheel has an encoder, which reports the speed of the wheel's rim. Call the three readings s1, s2, s3.

Each wheel writes one equation

Put wheel i on a circle of radius R around the robot's centre, at the angle αi, rolling along the tangent to the circle. Its reading is

si = −sin(αi) vx + cos(αi) vy + R ω

The reason is short. A wheel only feels motion along its rolling direction, and that direction is the tangent (−sin αi, cos αi). So it picks up that share of vx and that share of vy. Turning the robot carries every wheel along its own tangent at the speed Rω, so each wheel picks up the same Rω.

Three wheels give three linear equations in three unknowns. Sensors write the rows, as they did in Lecture 1.

Wheels at 90°, 210° and 330°, radius R = 0.2 m
vxvyω = s1s2s3

Elimination needs no row exchange here. The pivots are −1, −0.87 and 0.6. The last row of U reads 0.6 ω = s1 + s2 + s3: add the three readings, divide by 3R, and you have the turn rate. Back-substitution then gives vy and vx.

With readings (−0.20, 0.25, 0.25) m/s, the sum is 0.30, so ω = 0.30 ÷ 0.6 = 0.5 rad/s. Then vy = 0 and vx = 0.3 m/s. The robot is driving round a circle of radius 0.6 m.

Three more layouts, three lessons

Wheels at 0°, 120°, 240°. This is the same robot with its wheels numbered from a different starting angle. Wheel 1 now rolls exactly along y, so its row is [0 1 0.2]. The first pivot position holds 0. Elimination exchanges rows 1 and 2 and carries on. This is the temporary failure, and its meaning is plain: wheel 1 knows nothing about vx, so ask a different wheel first.

Nearly parallel. Mount the wheels so that they all roll almost along x, fanned apart by only 8°. A second pivot exists, but it is small (0.14). The answer exists too, but it is fragile, as the warning in section 2.3 said it would be.

All parallel. Now mount all three wheels to roll exactly along x. A wheel at height yi reads vx − yiω, so its row is [1 0 −yi]. The whole vy column is zero. Elimination finds no second pivot and nothing to exchange in. This is the permanent failure: the robot can slide sideways and no encoder will notice.

Odometry: solve, step, repeat

A robot repeats this calculation many times a second. Each time, it solves for its body velocity, multiplies by the short time since the last reading, and adds that small motion to its running estimate of where it is. Tracking position by adding up small measured motions is called odometry, or dead reckoning.

Robotics linkdead reckoning

What a real robot adds to this

The model here is an ideal one: a flat floor, perfectly round wheels and no slipping. On a real floor the wheels slip a little. Each small error is added into the position estimate and never taken out again, so odometry slowly drifts. Robots therefore combine it with sensors that look at the outside world, such as the beacons of Lecture 1.

The linear algebra does not change. The computer still builds a matrix from the wheel layout, still eliminates, and still treats a missing or tiny pivot as a warning about the hardware.

Key idea

Elimination questions the sensors one at a time. A zero pivot is a sensor with nothing to say about the unknown in question. If another sensor can answer, exchange rows. If none can, the robot is blind in that direction.

From the bottom: 3z = 9 so z = 3. Then 2y + 3 = 7 so y = 2. Then x + 2 + 3 = 6 so x = 1.

Answer: multipliers 2, 3, 2; pivots 1, 2, 3; solution (1, 2, 3).

P3Run elimination on (a) and (b) . Each one hits a zero in a pivot position. Which failure is temporary and which is permanent?failure

(a) Row 2 − 2 × row 1 is [0 0 3]. The second pivot position holds 0, but the entry below it is 3. Exchange rows 2 and 3 and the matrix is upper triangular, with rows [1 2 1], [0 3 1], [0 0 3].

(b) Row 2 − 2 × row 1 is [0 0 3] and row 3 − 3 × row 1 is [0 0 1]. The second pivot position holds 0 and so does the entry below it. There is nothing to exchange in.

Answer: (a) is temporary: one row exchange, pivots 1, 3, 3. (b) is permanent: the matrix is singular. You could have seen it coming, because its second column is twice its first.

P7A field robot has two wheels 2 m apart. The left encoder reads 1 m/s and the right reads 3 m/s. Find its forward speed and turn rate by elimination. Then say how much a 0.1 m/s error on the right encoder changes the turn rate.robot

With W = 2 the wheel equations are v − ω = 1 and v + ω = 3.

→

Multiplier 1. Row 2 − row 1 gives 2ω = 2, so ω = 1. Then v − 1 = 1, so v = 2.

Answer: 2 m/s forward, turning at 1 rad/s. The pivots are 1 and 2, and the second pivot is the wheel spacing. An error of 0.1 m/s is divided by that pivot, so the turn rate changes by 0.05 rad/s.

Follow-up: put the wheels 0.2 m apart. The second pivot is 0.2 and the same error changes the turn rate by 0.5 rad/s, ten times as much.

P8An omni robot of radius 1 m has its wheels at 0°, 90° and 180°. The encoders read (s1, s2, s3) = (5, 2, 1) m/s. Write the three wheel equations and find the body velocity. What must elimination do before it can start, and why?robot

Each row is [−sin α, cos α, R] with R = 1. For 0° that is [0 1 1], for 90° it is [−1 0 1], for 180° it is [0 −1 1].

→→

The first pivot position holds 0, because wheel 1 rolls along y and feels nothing of vx. Exchange rows 1 and 2. Then row 3 + row 2 gives 2ω = 6.

Back-substitute: ω = 3. Then vy + 3 = 5, so vy = 2. Then −vx + 3 = 2, so vx = 1.

Answer: (vx, vy, ω) = (1, 2, 3), after one row exchange. The pivots are −1, 1 and 2. The robot is healthy. Only the order of the questions had to change.

P9The robot of P8 is moving in exactly the same way, but a technician has plugged the cables of encoders 1 and 2 into each other's sockets. The computer now receives (2, 5, 1) and still uses the old matrix. What does it report? Which matrix repairs the software: a P on the left of A, or on the right?robot

With the old matrix and the right-hand side (2, 5, 1), the row exchange gives [−1 0 1 | 5], [0 1 1 | 2], [0 −1 1 | 1]. Row 3 + row 2: 2ω = 3. So ω = 1.5, vy = 0.5 and vx = 1.5 − 5 = −3.5.

The computer believes the robot is reversing at 3.5 m/s. The true motion is still (1, 2, 3).

The readings arrive in a different order, so the equations must be listed in that order too. Re-ordering equations is a row exchange: use PA, with P exchanging rows 1 and 2. Solving PAx = (2, 5, 1) gives (1, 2, 3) again.

Answer: it reports (−3.5, 0.5, 1.5). The cure is P on the left. A P on the right would re-order the unknowns, which is a different change: it would swap the meanings of vx and vy.

P10A cart has three wheels that all roll along x, mounted at heights y = 1, 0, −1. A wheel at height y reads vx − yω. Which motion can the encoders not see? Do the readings (1, 2, 3) fit a rigid motion? Do (1, 2, 4)? Suggest a repair.robot

The vy column is all zeros. After step (2,1) and step (3,1) the second pivot position holds 0 with a 0 below it: permanent failure. Sliding sideways turns no wheel.

Readings (1, 2, 3). Row 2 − row 1 says ω = 1. Row 3 − row 1 says 2ω = 2. They agree. So ω = 1 and vx = 2, while vy can be anything: infinitely many solutions.

Readings (1, 2, 4). Now the rows say ω = 1 and 2ω = 3. They contradict each other, so no rigid motion fits. A wheel is slipping or a sensor is faulty.

Answer: sideways motion is invisible; (1, 2, 3) is consistent with vx = 2, ω = 1 and unknown vy; (1, 2, 4) is impossible.

Follow-up: turn the middle wheel to roll along y. Its row becomes [0 1 0], the pivots become 1, 1, 2, and the readings (1, 2, 3) then give exactly one motion, (vx, vy, ω) = (2, 2, 1).

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each one.

Q1In the lecture's matrix, which multiplier clears the entry in row 2, column 1, and how do you find it?

The multiplier is 3. It is the entry to remove divided by the pivot: 3 ÷ 1 = 3. Subtracting 3 × row 1 from row 2 turns [3 8 1] into [0 2 −2].

Q2Elimination has produced x + 2y + z = 2, 2y − 2z = 6, 5z = −10. Solve it, and say why you start with the last equation.

The last equation has only one unknown, so it can be solved at once: z = −2. Put that into the second: 2y + 4 = 6, so y = 1. Put both into the first: x + 2 − 2 = 2, so x = 2. The answer is (2, 1, −2).

Q3Change the bottom-right entry of A from 1 to −4. What happens during elimination, and what is such a matrix called?

The last step gives [0 4 −4] − 2 × [0 2 −2] = [0 0 0]. The third pivot position holds 0 and there is no row below to exchange with. Elimination fails permanently. The matrix is singular, or not invertible. With the lecture's right-hand side the last row reads 0 = −10, so there is no solution.

Q4Write the matrix that subtracts 3 × row 1 from row 2 of a 3 by 3 matrix. Then write the matrix that undoes it.

E21 is the identity with −3 in row 2, column 1: rows [1 0 0], [−3 1 0], [0 0 1]. Its inverse adds the same amount back, so it has +3 in that position: rows [1 0 0], [3 1 0], [0 0 1]. Their product is the identity.

Q5E32 E21 has a 6 in its bottom-left corner. E21 E32 has a 0 there. Why are they different?

In E32 E21, step (2,1) acts first. Row 2 already contains −3 of row 1 by the time step (3,2) subtracts twice row 2 from row 3, and (−2) × (−3) = 6. In the other order, step (3,2) acts first on the untouched row 2, so no row 1 reaches row 3. Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative.

Q6You multiply a 2 by 2 matrix by P, the identity with its rows exchanged, on the right. What happens, and what would that be in a robot?

The two columns trade places. Columns belong to unknowns, so this re-orders the unknowns, for example re-labelling two axes or numbering two motors the other way round. On the left, the same P would exchange the rows, which re-orders the equations, that is, the sensors.

Q7An omni robot has wheels at 0°, 120° and 240°. The first row of its matrix is [0 1 R]. What does the zero say, and what does elimination do about it? What if all three wheels were parallel?

The zero says that wheel 1 rolls along y and feels nothing of vx. The first pivot position is zero, so elimination exchanges row 1 with a row below it and goes on normally. With three parallel wheels, the whole vy column is zero. No row can supply the second pivot, the matrix is singular, and sideways sliding cannot be detected by any encoder.

For your article

One-line summary: elimination solves a system by removing one unknown at a time, and every step of it is a matrix that can be stored, multiplied together and undone.

A robot sentence you can reuse: elimination questions the sensors one by one; a zero pivot is a sensor with nothing to say, and a singular matrix is a motion that no sensor can see.
