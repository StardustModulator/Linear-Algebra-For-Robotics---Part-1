Multiplication and Inverse Matrices

Multiplying two matrices means doing one thing after another. Inverting a matrix means undoing it. The lecture gives five ways to multiply and one clean way to invert. A robot uses both all day: the pose of an arm is a product of matrices, and changing viewpoint is an inverse.

Watch the lecture on OCW Lecture summary Article notes
3.0

Start here: one thing after another

You need one idea from Lecture 1: a matrix times a vector is a mix of the matrix's columns. Nothing else.

Think of a matrix as a machine. A list of numbers goes in, the matrix multiplies it, and a new list comes out. Lecture 1 wrote this as Ax.

Now put two machines in a row. Whatever comes out of the first one, B, is fed straight into the second one, A. The pair behaves like a single bigger machine, and that machine is again a matrix. It is called the product of the two and is written AB. Read it from the right: B acts first, then A.

Some machines can be run backwards. The matrix that takes every output of A back to the input it came from is called the inverse of A, written A−1 and read "A inverse".

The lecture answers two questions.

  • How do you work out AB? Professor Strang gives five ways. They always produce the same numbers, but each one shows you something different about the product.
  • When does a matrix have an inverse, and how do you find it? The answer to "when" is the word singular from Lecture 1. The answer to "how" is a method called Gauss-Jordan elimination, which is Lecture 2's elimination taken one step further.

One warning before starting, because it is used all the way through: AB is usually not the same as BA. The order of the machines matters. Section 3.6 shows why.

Robotics linkposes and frames

A robot's pose is a matrix

A robot has to say where things are, and "where" always means "measured from something". A frame is that something: a starting point called the origin, plus two arrows at right angles called the x axis and the y axis. One frame is fixed to the floor and called the world frame. Another is fixed to the robot, with x pointing forward and y pointing to the robot's left.

Say a rover stands at the world point (x, y) and faces the direction θ, an angle measured anticlockwise from the world x axis. Position and direction together are the rover's pose. A rock that the rover sees a metres ahead and b metres to its left sits at this place in the world:

xworld = (cos θ) a − (sin θ) b + x   yworld = (sin θ) a + (cos θ) b + y

Here cos θ and sin θ are just two numbers. They say how much of one step in the direction θ goes along the world x axis and how much along the world y axis. At 0° they are 1 and 0. At 90° they are 0 and 1.

Roboticists pack both lines into one matrix by giving every point a third entry that is always 1:

=

This 3 by 3 matrix T is called a homogeneous transform. It holds the whole pose, and multiplying by it converts rover coordinates into world coordinates. The extra 1 is a bookkeeping trick that lets one multiplication both turn and shift. This page stays in the flat plane. A robot working in three dimensions uses the same idea with 4 by 4 matrices.

Example. A rover stands at (3, 1) and faces 90°, straight up the page. A rock lies 2 m ahead of it, so the rock is at (2, 0) in the rover's frame.

The rock is at (3, 3) in the world: 2 m further up the page than the rover, as it should be. Keep this rover in mind. The rest of the page multiplies matrices like T to chain frames together, and inverts them to change viewpoint.

3.1

Way 1: one entry at a time

The rule most people learn first: a row times a column.

Write the product as AB = C. Each number inside a matrix is called an entry, and it is named by its row and then its column: a32 is the entry of A in row 3, column 2.

The standard rule builds C one entry at a time. For the entry in row i and column j, take row i of A and column j of B. Multiply their first numbers together, their second numbers together, and so on. Add the results. This row-times-column sum is the dot product from Lecture 1.

The lecture's entry
c34=(row 3 of A) · (column 4 of B)=a31b14 + a32b24 + a33b34 + ⋯

Strang chooses row 3 and column 4 so that nobody mistakes the pattern for something special about a corner. Follow the small numbers. The 3 stays fixed on every a. The 4 stays fixed on every b. The inside numbers run 1, 2, 3, … together.

The size rule

A row can only be multiplied into a column that holds the same number of entries. A row of A has as many entries as A has columns. A column of B has as many entries as B has rows. So the number of columns of A must equal the number of rows of B.

Sizes are always given as rows by columns. If A is m by n and B is n by p, then AB is m by p. Write the two sizes side by side, (m × n)(n × p). The inner numbers must match, and then they drop out. The outer numbers are the size of the answer.

Our pair is (3 × 2)(2 × 2), so the product is 3 × 2. Now try the other order: BA would be (2 × 2)(3 × 2). The inner numbers are 2 and 3. They do not match, so BA does not exist. That is the first sign that order matters.

Robotics linkwiring

The size rule is a wiring rule: frames must chain

Every robot matrix converts one list of numbers into another. A matrix that turns the 3 numbers of a body velocity into 4 wheel speeds is 4 by 3, and it can only be fed by something that produces 3 numbers. The size rule is the check an electrician makes: the plug has to fit the socket.

Pose matrices are all 3 by 3, so their sizes always fit. Robot programmers therefore apply a stricter version of the rule to the frames. Name each transform by the frame it converts to and the frame it converts from. Tworld←rover turns rover coordinates into world coordinates. Trover←camera turns camera coordinates into rover coordinates. Then

Tworld←roverTrover←camera=Tworld←camera

The two inner names match and drop out, exactly like the inner sizes. The frame that the right-hand matrix delivers must be the frame that the left-hand matrix expects. A product such as Tworld←roverTworld←camera passes the size check and is still nonsense. Writing the frame names on every transform is how careful robot code catches that mistake.

3.2

Way 2: a whole column at a time

Each column of AB is A times a column of B.

Lecture 1 showed that a matrix times a vector is a combination of the matrix's columns: stretch each column by a number, then add. The second way to multiply matrices uses nothing more than that.

Cut B into its columns. Multiply A by each one. Stand the answers side by side and you have C:

column j of AB=A × (column j of B)

So every column of AB is a combination of the columns of A. Column j of B holds the amounts.

Our pair, by columns
1+1=      6+2=

The amounts 1 and 1 are the first column of B. The amounts 6 and 2 are its second column. The two amber results are the two columns of C.

Why the rule is what it is

Matrix multiplication was designed so that the product does the job of both machines. Feeding x through B and then through A must give the same result as feeding it through AB once:

A(Bx)=(AB)x

Check it with x = (1, 1). First Bx = (7, 3), then A times (7, 3) is (35, 45, 55). The short way: AB times (1, 1) adds the two columns of C, which is (9 + 26, 11 + 34, 13 + 42) = (35, 45, 55).

Robotics linkforward kinematics

The pose of a hand is a product, and its columns are the hand's frame

Take a flat arm with two links. Fix a frame to each moving part: one to link 1 at the shoulder, one to link 2 at the elbow, one to the hand. Each frame's x axis points along its link. Three small transforms describe the arm, and each one relates a frame to the frame before it.

  • T1, link 1 seen from the world: turn by the shoulder angle θ1.
  • T2, link 2 seen from link 1: move the length of link 1 along it, then turn by the elbow angle θ2.
  • T3, the hand seen from link 2: move the length of link 2 along it.

The pose of the hand in the world is the product T = T1T2T3. Finding the hand from the joint angles is called forward kinematics. For every serial arm, with any number of joints, it is a chain of matrix products like this one.

3.3

Way 3: a whole row at a time

The mirror image: each row of AB is a row of A times B.

Everything in the last section can be turned on its side. Cut A into its rows. Multiply each row by B. Stack the answers and you have C:

row i of AB=(row i of A) × B

A row times a matrix is a combination of the matrix's rows. So every row of AB is a combination of the rows of B. Row i of A holds the amounts.

Our pair, by rows
2+7=     3+8=     4+9=

The amounts 2 and 7 are row 1 of A, then 3 and 8 are row 2, then 4 and 9 are row 3.

Put ways 2 and 3 side by side and you can read a product from both ends. The columns of AB are combinations of the columns of A. The rows of AB are combinations of the rows of B.

You met the row way in Lecture 2 without its name. The step "subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2" is done by multiplying on the left with a matrix whose second row is (−2, 1). The new row 2 is −2 of row 1 plus 1 of row 2: a combination of rows. Section 3.9 leans on this.

Way 4: columns times rows

Robotics linka one-trick controller

A column times a row is a one-trick machine

Call the column d and the row w, and let the matrix be K = d w. Feed any vector e into K. The row acts first and boils e down to a single number. The column then stretches itself by that number. Whatever goes in, the output points along d. Only its size changes.

Picture a hovering robot with one side thruster that can push only along the direction d = (1, 2). Its controller reads a position error e and commands the push Ke, with w = [1 3]:

K==

An error of (2, 1) gives the number 1·2 + 3·1 = 5, so the push is 5 times d, which is (5, 10). An error of (3, −1) gives 1·3 + 3·(−1) = 0: no push at all. This controller listens along one direction and pushes along one direction. It is blind to any error at right angles to w, and it can never push off the line of d.

Robot papers write this as K = dwT. The small T, for transpose, only means "w laid on its side as a row", and Lecture 5 covers it. Remember this matrix. It returns in section 3.8 as the lecture's example of a matrix that cannot be undone.

3.5

Way 5: block by block

Cut big matrices into smaller ones and multiply the pieces.

The last way is a piece of bookkeeping. Draw lines through A and B to cut each into smaller matrices called blocks. Then multiply as if every block were a single number:

The lecture's block rule
=

The top-left block of the answer is "row 1 times column 1", exactly as it would be for numbers, except that each product is now a matrix product.

Two cautions. The cuts must fit: every block product, such as A1B1, has to pass the size rule, and that happens when the column cuts of A match the row cuts of B. And the order inside each product must be kept: A1B1, never B1A1.

The earlier ways are block multiplication with particular cuts. Cut B into its columns and leave A whole, and you have way 2. Cut A into its rows and leave B whole, and you have way 3.

Robotics linkhow robot code is written

This is how robot software multiplies poses

A pose matrix has a natural cut. The top-left 2 by 2 block is the turning part, a rotation matrix R. The top-right block is the position p. The bottom row is always 0 0 1. Multiply two poses block by block:

=

Top-left: RR′ + p·0 = RR′. Top-right: Rp′ + p·1. Bottom row: 0 and 1 again. Two plain rules come out.

  • Rotations compose with rotations. The new turning part is RR′ and nothing else.
  • Positions add after being rotated. The second position p′ is measured in the first frame, so it is turned by R before it is added to p.

Robot libraries store only R and p and use this formula directly. The bottom row never changes, so multiplying it out would be wasted work. Check it on the arm of Lab 3.2: T1T2 has turning part "30° then 60°", which is a turn of 90°, and position R1(2, 0) + (0, 0) = (1.73, 1.00). That is the elbow. In three dimensions R is 3 by 3, p has three entries, and the block formula is unchanged.

3.6

Order matters: AB is usually not BA

The one habit from ordinary arithmetic that you have to drop.

With numbers, 3 × 5 and 5 × 3 are the same. With matrices the two orders are different questions, and three things can go wrong.

  • BA may not exist. Our 3 by 2 and 2 by 2 pair multiplies in one order only.
  • BA may have a different size. A 2 by 3 matrix times a 3 by 2 matrix is 2 by 2. In the other order it is 3 by 3.
  • Even when both are square and the same size, the numbers usually differ.
Same two matrices, two orders
=      =

Multiplying by P on the left works on rows (way 3) and exchanges the two rows of A. Multiplying by P on the right works on columns (way 2) and exchanges the two columns. Different jobs, different answers.

Mathematicians say matrix multiplication is not commutative. One freedom does survive. You may move the brackets, (AB)C = A(BC), as long as the matrices keep their left-to-right order. That is why a chain such as T1T2T3 needs no brackets.

A few special pairs do commute, and the most important is a matrix and its own inverse. That is the next section.

3.7

The inverse: a matrix that undoes another

Square matrices only, from here to the end of the lecture.

For numbers, multiplying by 5 is undone by multiplying by 1/5, because (1/5) × 5 = 1 and multiplying by 1 changes nothing.

The matrix that changes nothing is the identity matrix I: ones down the diagonal and zeros everywhere else. Ix = x for every vector, and IA = A = AI for every matrix that fits.

The inverse of a square matrix A is the matrix A−1 that brings you back to I:

A−1A=I=AA−1

The left half says A−1 undoes A. The right half says A undoes A−1. Since order matters, these are two separate claims. Strang states a fact that is far from obvious and takes real work to prove: for a square matrix, an inverse that works on the left also works on the right. A square matrix and its inverse are one of the rare pairs that commute.

A matrix that has an inverse is called invertible or non-singular. Lecture 1 used the same words for a system with exactly one solution, and they mean the same thing here: if Ax = b and the inverse exists, multiply both sides by it and the only possible solution is x = A−1b.

Robotics linkundo

Undo a move, or change your point of view

Undo. The command D drove the rover 2 m forward. Its inverse drives it 2 m backward, and together they leave the rover where it began:

=

The inverse of the turn R is a turn of 90° the other way. To undo a chain of moves, undo the last one first, as you take off shoes before socks: (RD)−1 = D−1R−1. Lecture 4 proves that rule.

Change viewpoint. The pose T turns "where the rover sees a thing" into "where the thing is in the world". Very often a robot needs the opposite. Its map says where a landmark is in the world, and it has to work out where that is from its own seat. That conversion is T−1. Section 3.10 builds it and Lab 3.5 lets you drive it.

Watch out

Do not compute A−1 just to solve one system Ax = b. Elimination reaches x with less work, and Lecture 4 counts the difference. Robots compute an inverse when the inverse itself means something, such as a viewpoint, or when it has a shortcut formula, as poses do.

3.8

The singular case: when there is no way back

Not every square matrix can be undone. Here is how to tell.

Some square matrices have no inverse. They are the singular matrices of Lecture 1, and the lecture puts one on the board to study.

The lecture's singular matrix
A=

Reason one: look at the columns. The second column (3, 6) is 3 times the first column (1, 2). Both lie on the same line through the origin. By way 2, every column of A times anything is a combination of these two columns, so it stays on that line. But AA−1 = I would need a column equal to (1, 0), the first column of I, and (1, 0) is not on the line. No matrix can do it.

Reason two, the one to remember: some vector is sent to zero. Take x = (3, −1).

=3−1=

Suppose an inverse existed. Multiply both sides of Ax = 0 by it. The left side becomes A−1Ax = x. The right side becomes A−10 = 0. Together they say x = 0. But x is (3, −1), which is not zero. The contradiction means the inverse cannot exist.

The same argument in plain words: this matrix sends (3, −1) to zero, and like every matrix it also sends (0, 0) to zero. Two different inputs, one output. A machine that ran A backwards would be handed that output and could not know which input to give back.

Key idea

If a matrix sends any non-zero vector to zero, it has no inverse. The vector it wipes out is the information it throws away.

3.9

Gauss-Jordan: finding the inverse

Solve for every column of the inverse at once.

Now take a matrix that does have an inverse, the lecture's A with rows (1, 3) and (2, 7), and find it. Way 2 turns the definition AA−1 = I into something familiar: A times column j of A−1 equals column j of I. Call the unknown columns (a, b) and (c, d).

Two systems with the same A

Lecture 2 solved one system like this by elimination. The row steps depend only on A, so the same steps solve both.

Gauss-Jordan elimination does both at once. Write A with both right-hand sides beside it. The two right-hand sides together are the identity, so the starting block is [ A | I ]. Then do row steps until the left half becomes I.

The lecture's elimination
→ →

First step, Gauss's part. Subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2. That clears the entry below the first pivot. The left half is now upper triangular, which is where Lecture 2 would stop.

Second step, Jordan's part. Keep going, upward. Subtract 3 times row 2 from row 1 to clear the entry above the second pivot.

The left half is now I and the right half is the inverse, with rows (7, −3) and (−2, 1). If a pivot were not 1, one more step would divide its row by it.

Why it works

Lecture 2 showed that every row step is a multiplication on the left by a small matrix. The first step above is E21 and the second is E12. Doing one and then the other is a single matrix, their product. Call it E.

All the row steps as one matrix
E==
E [ A | I ]=[ EA | EI ]=[ I | E ]

The second line is block multiplication: E multiplies both halves at once. The steps were chosen so that the left half became I. That is the statement EA = I, and it says E is A−1. Meanwhile the right half started as I and became EI = E. So the inverse appears exactly where the identity used to be.

Gauss-Jordan does not search for the inverse. It builds it, one row step at a time, by doing to I everything it does to A.

3.10

The robot: changing viewpoint

Product, blocks and inverse, all working on one rover.

The rover's pose T converts rover coordinates into world coordinates. Its inverse converts the other way. A pose can always be undone, and block multiplication gives the inverse with no elimination at all.

Think about what T does to a point: it turns the point by R, then shifts it by p. To undo that, shift back by −p and then turn back. Turning back is the inverse of the rotation, and a rotation matrix has a very cheap inverse: swap its rows with its columns. The swapped matrix is called the transpose and written RT. Lecture 5 explains why this works. Here we check it by multiplying.

Robotics linkviewpoint

"Where is that landmark, as seen from me?"

Maps are stored in world coordinates. Decisions are made in robot coordinates: steer left or right, reach forward or back. Every time a robot looks something up on its map and acts on it, a T−1 sits in between.

The rows of T−1 say what it does. Its first row holds the rover's own x axis, so it measures how far along that axis the landmark lies: the "ahead" number. Its second row does the same for the rover's y axis: the "left" number. The two columns of R have become the two rows of RT. That is the whole reason a transpose appears.

Chains of these are everyday robot arithmetic. A camera on the hand sees a cup. Where is the cup from the robot's base? Multiply Tbase←handThand←camera into the cup's camera coordinates. The map knows the cup in the world. Where is it from the hand? Multiply Tworld←hand inverse into its world coordinates.

P7The rover of P6 drives 3 m forward and then turns 90° to the left. Use the block rule to find its new pose. Then swap the two commands and find the pose again.robot

Each command multiplies the pose on the right. The block rule says the new rotation is RR′ and the new position is Rp′ + p.

Drive, then turn. Driving has no turning part and p′ = (3, 0). The rover's R turns (3, 0) into (0, 3), so the position becomes (0, 3) + (2, 1) = (2, 4). Turning on the spot then changes only the heading, from 90° to 180°.

Turn, then drive. The turn makes the heading 180° and leaves the rover at (2, 1). Now R is a half turn and sends (3, 0) to (−3, 0), so the position becomes (−3, 0) + (2, 1) = (−1, 1).

Answer: drive-then-turn ends at (2, 4) facing 180°. Turn-then-drive ends at (−1, 1) facing 180°. Same heading, places 4.24 m apart.

Repair. Turning needs the wheels to differ, so the turn stick must enter with opposite signs: left = forward − turn, right = forward + turn.

→→→

The steps: subtract row 1 from row 2, add half of row 2 to row 1, divide row 2 by 2. Column 1 of the inverse is the stick position for "left wheel 1, right wheel 0".

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each one.

Q1A is 3 by 2 and B is 2 by 4. What size is AB? Does BA exist?

AB is (3 × 2)(2 × 4). The inner sizes match, so the product exists and is 3 by 4. BA would be (2 × 4)(3 × 2). The inner sizes 4 and 3 differ, so it does not exist.

Q2The columns of AB are combinations of the columns of which matrix? The rows of AB are combinations of the rows of which matrix?

The columns of AB are combinations of the columns of A, with amounts taken from the columns of B. The rows of AB are combinations of the rows of B, with amounts taken from the rows of A.

Q3Why is every row of the column (2, 3, 4) times the row [1 6] a multiple of [1 6]? What does such a matrix do as a machine?

Row i of the result is the i-th number of the column times the whole row, so the rows are 2, 3 and 4 times [1 6]. As a machine it turns any input into one number, using the row, and then stretches the column by that number. Every output lies along the column (2, 3, 4).

Q4A rover turns 90° left and then drives 2 m. A second rover drives 2 m and then turns 90° left. Where does each end, and what matrix fact is this?

Starting at the origin facing along x, the first ends at (0, 2) and the second at (2, 0). Both face 90°. The poses are the products RD and DR, and they differ: matrix multiplication is not commutative.

Q5Give two reasons why the matrix with rows (1, 3) and (2, 6) has no inverse.

First, its columns (1, 2) and (3, 6) lie on one line, so every combination of them does too, and (1, 0), a column of I, can never be produced. Second, it sends the non-zero vector (3, −1) to zero. If an inverse existed, multiplying Ax = 0 by it would force x = 0, which is false.

Q6In Gauss-Jordan, why does the right half of [ A | I ] end up holding A−1?

All the row steps together are one matrix E multiplying on the left: E[ A | I ] = [ EA | E ]. The steps are chosen to make the left half I, so EA = I and E is the inverse. The right half is EI = E, the same matrix.

Q7A rover is at (3, 1) facing 90°. A landmark is at (5, 1) on the map. Where is it as the rover sees it?

Use the T−1 from section 3.10, with rows (0, 1, −1) and (−1, 0, 3). Ahead: 0·5 + 1·1 − 1 = 0. Left: −1·5 + 0·1 + 3 = −2. The landmark is level with the rover and 2 m to its right. That fits the picture: the rover faces up the page and the landmark is 2 m to the east.

For your article

One-line summary: a matrix product is one action followed by another, an inverse is the action that takes it back, and a matrix with no inverse has thrown information away.

A robot sentence you can reuse: forward kinematics multiplies, changing viewpoint inverts, and a singular matrix is a sensor or a joint that has lost a direction.
