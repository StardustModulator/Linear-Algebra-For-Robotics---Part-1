One robot, seven lectures

Every idea in the first seven lectures describes the same machine. Here is one arm with three joints doing a job that needs only two. Switch between the seven lenses and watch the same robot, in the same pose, become a column picture, an elimination, a product of matrices, an LU solve, a transpose, a column space with a nullspace, and finally a rank.

The machine

A planar arm with three joints that moves its hand in a plane.

The arm has links of length 1.6, 1.3 and 1.0 and three joints. Its hand moves in a flat plane, so the job has two numbers (the hand's velocity across and up) while the arm has three joint speeds to choose. One joint more than the job needs: the arm is redundant.

Everything below comes from one matrix, the Jacobian J. It has 2 rows (the job) and 3 columns (the joints), and it turns joint speeds into hand velocity: J q̇ = v. Each lens reads that one matrix with the idea of one lecture.

One arm, seven ways of seeing it

Pick a lens. Drag the amber handle at the hand to change the wanted velocity (or, in lens 05, the force on the hand). Pose the arm with the sliders, or press Trace a path and let it move on its own while every lens stays live.

Try this
  1. Stay on lens 06 and press Self-motion. The elbow swings while the hand does not move: that is the nullspace. Then switch to lens 07, still moving, and see the same motion appear as the special solution.
  2. Drag the second and third sliders to 0° so the arm is straight. Visit every lens: the columns line up (01), a pivot disappears (02, 07), the column space collapses to a line (06), and the rank drops from 2 to 1 (07). One event, seven descriptions.
  3. On lens 05, point the force straight along the stretched arm. The joint torques fall to almost nothing, because the links carry the load.

Key idea

The seven lectures are not seven topics. They are seven questions asked of one matrix: what does it combine, how do we solve it, how does it compose, how do we solve it fast, what does it do to forces, what can it reach and miss, and how many directions does it really have?

Honest about the model

The arm is planar, rigid and ideal: no gravity, no friction, no joint limits. When it traces a path it uses the smallest joint speeds that do the job, with a very small amount of damping so that it stays calm near a straight pose. Real arm controllers add limits and dynamics on top of exactly this linear algebra.
