Factorization into A = LU

Elimination, done once, can be saved as two triangular matrices, L and U. After that, every new right-hand side is cheap. A robot controller that solves the same system a thousand times a second lives on this.

4.0

Start here: can elimination be saved?

What this lecture is for, and the four ideas from earlier lectures that it leans on.

Lecture 2 solved Ax = b by elimination: subtract multiples of one equation from the equations below it until the system is easy to finish. Elimination is real work. This lecture asks a practical question: can we do that work once and keep it?

The answer is yes. The record of elimination is two matrices, called L and U, and multiplied together they give back A. Writing a matrix as a product of simpler matrices is called a factorization, in the same way that 12 = 3 × 4 factors a number.

You need four ideas from the earlier lectures. Here they are again in one line each.

  • A system Ax = b has a matrix A of known coefficients, a vector x of unknowns and a right-hand side b of known numbers.
  • A pivot is the entry on the diagonal that elimination uses to clear the entries below it. A multiplier says how many times the pivot row is subtracted from a lower row: the entry to be cleared, divided by the pivot.
  • An elimination matrix such as E21 carries out one of those subtractions when it multiplies A. The subscript says which entry it clears: row 2, column 1.
  • The inverse A−1 is the matrix that undoes A, so A−1A = I. Here I is the identity matrix, with ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Multiplying by it changes nothing.

Strang's lecture has four parts, and this page follows them: how to undo a product of matrices, elimination rewritten as A = LU, what elimination costs, and what to do when rows have to be exchanged.

Robotics linkreal-time control

A controller is a loop with a deadline

A robot's controller is a short program that repeats without end: read the sensors, work out the motor commands, send them. This is a control loop, and one pass through it is a tick. Arms and legged robots often run the loop about a thousand times a second, which is one kilohertz (1 kHz). Each tick then has one millisecond for all of its arithmetic.

Part of that arithmetic is usually solving Ax = b. Very often the matrix stays the same from tick to tick and only the right-hand side changes, because the right-hand side carries the fresh sensor readings. Repeating elimination on the same matrix every tick would be wasted effort. The plan of this lecture is the plan of the controller: factor once before the loop starts, and leave only the cheap part inside the loop.

4.1

Undoing a product: reverse order

The inverse of AB is B−1A−1. The order flips.

Suppose A and B each have an inverse. What undoes their product AB?

Think about what the product does. In ABx the matrix next to the vector acts first, so B acts and then A acts. To get back to x you take back the most recent action first: undo A, then undo B.

The rule, and the lecture's check
(AB)−1 = B−1A−1

Check it by multiplying. The brackets may be moved, because matrix multiplication allows regrouping (Lecture 3). The matrices may not change places.

(AB)(B−1A−1)=A (B B−1) A−1=A I A−1=A A−1=I

The inner pair B B−1 cancels first, and then the outer pair. The same happens on the other side: (B−1A−1)(AB) = I.

Strang's picture for this is one everybody already knows. You put on socks, then shoes. To undo that, the shoes come off first, then the socks. Nobody takes socks off through their shoes.

The rule extends to any number of factors: (ABC)−1 = C−1B−1A−1. Section 4.4 uses exactly this with three elimination matrices.

Robotics linkundoing motion

Every move is a matrix, so undoing moves is undoing a product

A rover on a flat floor has a position (x, y) and a heading θ, the direction it faces. Robotics packs all three numbers into one 3 by 3 matrix called a homogeneous transform:

A move such as "drive 3 m" or "turn 70° and drive 2.5 m" is a matrix of the same shape. Moves described from the driver's seat chain from left to right: doing move A and then move B is the product AB. To return to the start the rover needs (AB)−1 = B−1A−1. It must take back B, the later move, first.

Get the order wrong and the rover ends up somewhere else, even though each undo move is correct in itself. The lab uses an ideal rover on a flat floor with no wheel slip, so that the only thing that can go wrong is the order.

Key idea

The inverse of a product is the product of the inverses in reverse order. Last on, first off.

4.2

Transposes and inverses

Invert and transpose in either order. The result is the same.

The transpose of a matrix, written AT, turns its rows into columns: row 1 of A becomes column 1 of AT, row 2 becomes column 2, and so on. Lecture 5 treats transposes in full. Today the lecture needs a single fact about them.

The rule, and the lecture's reason
(AT)−1 = (A−1)T

Start from the definition of the inverse, A A−1 = I, and transpose both sides. Transposing a product reverses its order, just as inverting does, and the identity is its own transpose:

(A−1)T AT = I

Read that line aloud: the matrix (A−1)T, multiplied by AT, gives the identity. So it is the inverse of AT.

Checked on the lecture's matrix

The amber matrix was found by inverting AT directly. It is also the second matrix with its rows turned into columns. Two routes, one answer.

Robotics linkmotion and force

One factorization serves velocities and forces

In Lecture 1 the arm's Jacobian J turned joint speeds into hand velocity. Lecture 5 will show that its transpose JT turns a force at the hand into torques at the joints. A controller often has to solve a system with J and a system with JT in the same tick.

The rule says these are one job. Whatever undoes J, transposed, undoes JT. In practice the controller factors J = LU once and reuses the same two factors for JT = UTLT. For the arm pose of Lecture 1 the script for this page finds the joint speeds (−0.37, 0.90) rad/s for a wanted hand velocity, and the hand force (−0.068, 0.320) N for joint torques of (1.0, 0.5) N·m, from one factorization.

4.3

A = LU for a 2 by 2, then A = LDU

Elimination, written as a product of two triangular matrices.

Now the main idea. Two words first. The diagonal of a square matrix runs from its top-left entry to its bottom-right entry. A matrix is upper triangular when every entry below the diagonal is zero, and lower triangular when every entry above the diagonal is zero.

Take the lecture's matrix and eliminate. There is one entry to clear, the 8.

The lecture's 2 by 2 example
  1. The first pivot is 2. The entry below it is 8. The multiplier is 8 ÷ 2 = 4, so subtract 4 × row 1 from row 2.
  2. As a matrix product, that step is E21 A = U:
=

U is upper triangular, and its diagonal holds the two pivots, 2 and 3. The letter U stands for upper.

  1. Move E21 to the other side by multiplying both sides by its inverse: A = E21−1 U.
  2. The inverse of "subtract 4 × row 1 from row 2" is "add 4 × row 1 to row 2". So E21−1 is the same matrix with +4 in place of −4. Call it L.
=

L is lower triangular, with ones on its diagonal. Below the diagonal sits the multiplier 4, with the sign we used when we said "subtract 4 times". The letter L stands for lower. Multiply the two matrices to check: row 2 of the product is 4·(2, 1) + (0, 3) = (8, 7).

Pulling the pivots out: A = LDU

Strang then tidies the picture. L has ones on its diagonal and U has pivots, which is lopsided. Divide each row of U by its pivot, and keep the pivots in a matrix of their own. A matrix whose only non-zero entries are on the diagonal is a diagonal matrix, here called D.

The same matrix as L D U
=

Row 1 of the old U was (2, 1). Divided by its pivot 2 it is (1, 1/2). Row 2 was (0, 3). Divided by 3 it is (0, 1). Now both triangular matrices have ones on the diagonal, and the pivots stand alone in the middle.

Robotics linka record to replay

L is the controller's logbook

Think of elimination as a job the controller did on the matrix. U is the finished job. L is the logbook: every multiplier that was used, written down in the place where it was used. With the logbook saved, the controller can replay the same steps on any new right-hand side without looking at A again. Section 4.5 shows the replay.

The LDU form matters to robots too. A robot's mass matrix, which links joint accelerations to joint torques, is symmetric: it equals its own transpose. For such a matrix the U in LDU turns out to be the transpose of L, so only L and D need storing. Lecture 5 introduces symmetric matrices.

4.4

The 3 by 3 case: why L beats E

Two matrices describe elimination. One of them is clean.

A 3 by 3 matrix has three entries below the diagonal, so elimination takes up to three steps: clear row 2, column 1, then row 3, column 1, then row 3, column 2. Assume for now that no rows have to be exchanged.

Three steps, then move them across
E32 E31 E21 A = U

Move the three matrices to the right-hand side one at a time. The last one applied, E32, is the first to be undone, so by the reverse-order rule of Section 4.1 the inverses come out in the opposite order:

A = E21−1 E31−1 E32−1 U = L U

So two matrices describe the whole of elimination. E = E32E31E21 takes A to U. Its inverse L takes U back to A. Strang asks which one is nicer to keep, and answers with an example.

The lecture's example

Suppose elimination needs two steps: subtract 2 × row 1 from row 2, and later subtract 5 × row 2 from row 3. Nothing is needed in row 3, column 1, so E31 = I.

=

Look at the 10. Neither step used a 10. It appears because the second step subtracts 5 × row 2 from row 3 after row 2 has already had 2 × row 1 taken out of it. So row 3 picks up 5 × 2 = 10 of row 1, passed along through row 2. Strang says plainly that he does not like that 10.

Now the other direction. The inverses multiply in the reverse order:

=

No 10. L holds the multipliers 2 and 5, each in its own place, and a 0 where no step was needed. Nothing has mixed.

Key idea

If there are no row exchanges, the multipliers go directly into L. There is nothing to compute: you write them down where you used them.

Why nothing mixes in L

Read A = LU one row at a time. Its third row says: row 3 of A equals l31 × (row 1 of U) plus l32 × (row 2 of U) plus row 3 of U. That sentence is elimination read backwards. Elimination produced row 3 of U by subtracting those same multiples from row 3 of A, and the rows it subtracted were always finished rows of U. Each multiplier is used once and recorded once.

Robotics linkmemory on board

Software keeps L in the space the zeros left behind

A numerical library never builds E, and it does not set aside a separate L either. Each time elimination turns an entry below the diagonal into zero, the library writes the multiplier into that now-unused slot. When it finishes, one n by n block of memory holds U on and above the diagonal and the multipliers of L below it. The ones on the diagonal of L are known in advance, so they are not stored.

That only works because the multipliers go into L unchanged. On a robot's small computer, a factorization that costs no extra memory is welcome. The timing code inside Lab 4.3 stores its factors this way.

4.5

Solving with L and U: two triangular passes

How the saved factors are used on a new right-hand side.

Once A = LU is known, the system Ax = b reads L(Ux) = b. Give the bracket a name, y = Ux, and one hard system becomes two easy ones.

  • First solve Ly = b. Because L is lower triangular, its first equation has one unknown, its second has one new unknown, and so on down. Working from the top like this is forward substitution.
  • Then solve Ux = y. Because U is upper triangular, start with the last equation and work upward. This is the back-substitution of Lecture 2.
The lecture's matrix with the right-hand side (3, 15)
=  then  =
  1. Forward, top row: y1 = 3. Second row: 4·3 + y2 = 15, so y2 = 3.
  2. Back, bottom row: 3x2 = 3, so x2 = 1. Top row: 2x1 + 1 = 3, so x1 = 1.
  3. Check in the original system: 2·1 + 1 = 3 and 8·1 + 7 = 15.

Now a new right-hand side arrives, (5, 23). L and U are not touched. Forward gives y = (5, 3), back gives x = (2, 1).

Forward substitution does to b exactly what elimination did to the rows of A: the vector y is the one Lecture 2 called c. This is the logbook being replayed. Notice also what never appeared on this page of working: A−1.

Robotics linkhow robot code is written

Robot software solves. It does not invert.

Formula sheets write the answer as x = A−1b. Robot code almost never computes A−1. Building the inverse costs about three times as much as finding L and U, and each use of it then costs the same as the two triangular passes. So the inverse is slower to get and no faster to use. It is usually less accurate as well, because every entry of A−1 carries its own rounding error into the product.

The standing advice in numerical computing is therefore: factor, then solve. When you see A−1b in a robotics paper, read it as "the solution of Ax = b", found with two triangular passes.

Key idea

Factor once: A = LU. Then for every new b, go forward with L and back with U. No inverse is ever formed.

4.6

How much does elimination cost?

About n³/3 operations for the matrix. Only n² for each right-hand side.

How long does elimination take on a big system? Strang measures it by counting operations. One operation is the basic act of elimination on a single entry: multiply a number and subtract it.

Take the lecture's size, n = 100: one hundred equations in one hundred unknowns. The first stage uses row 1 to clear column 1. It changes 99 rows, and about 100 entries in each, so it costs about 100² operations. The second stage does the same on what is left, a 99 by 99 block, for about 99². Then 98², and so on down to 1².

The lecture's count
n² + (n − 1)² + (n − 2)² + … + 1²≈n³ / 3

Why a third of n³? Strang compares the sum with the area under the curve x² from 0 to n, which calculus gives as n³/3. If you have not met calculus, check it with numbers. For n = 100 the sum of squares is 338,350 and n³/3 is 333,333. So elimination on a 100 by 100 matrix costs about a third of a million operations.

The right-hand side is far cheaper. While A is being eliminated, b needs one operation for each multiplier, about n²/2 in all. Back-substitution needs about n²/2 more. Together: n².

Exact counts (counted in a real run by the script for this page)
factor A: (n³ − n) / 3  each right-hand side: n²

For n = 100 these are 333,300 and 10,000. Once A = LU is known, each extra b costs n² where a fresh start would cost n³/3 + n²: about 34 times less work. And the gap widens with size. Double n and the factoring cost goes up 8 times, while the cost per right-hand side goes up 4 times.

4.7

The robot: one factorization, many right-hand sides

A smooth path through waypoints, re-planned for almost nothing.

Here is a robot problem in which the matrix truly never changes. A planner hands the robot a list of waypoints, points it must pass through in order. Joining them with straight lines would work, but the robot would have to stop and turn at every corner.

Robots prefer a spline: a chain of smooth curve pieces, one for each gap, joined so that position, velocity and acceleration all match where two pieces meet. Each piece here is a cubic, the simplest kind of curve that is flexible enough to allow this.

Give the robot one second for each gap. The whole spline is then fixed by one number per waypoint and per coordinate: the acceleration Mi as the robot passes waypoint i. Asking for the velocity to match at each inner waypoint gives one linear equation per inner waypoint:

The spline equations (derived in the article notes)
Mi−1 + 4Mi + Mi+1 = 6 (yi−1 − 2yi + yi+1)

Here yi is one coordinate of waypoint i. The path starts and ends with zero acceleration, M0 = Mn = 0, a choice known as the natural spline. For seven waypoints that leaves five unknown accelerations and five equations:

=

Look at what T contains: fours and ones. No waypoint coordinate appears in it. The coordinates are all on the right-hand side.

So T depends only on how many waypoints there are. One factorization T = LU serves the x coordinates, the y coordinates, and every later edit of the path. Moving a waypoint changes three entries of the right-hand side and nothing else.

There is a second gift. T is tridiagonal: its only non-zero entries are on the diagonal and its two neighbours. Elimination on such a matrix never creates a new non-zero, so L and U have just two diagonals each. A matrix whose non-zeros hug the diagonal like this is called banded, and for a banded matrix the two passes cost about 3n operations where a full matrix needs n².

The factors of T, and where the pivots go
pivots: 4,  15/4 = 3.75,  56/15 ≈ 3.7333,  209/56 ≈ 3.7321,  780/209 ≈ 3.7321

Each pivot is 4 minus 1 over the pivot before it, and the multipliers are 1 over each pivot: 1/4, 4/15, 15/56, 56/209. The pivots settle almost at once on the number that reproduces itself under that rule, p = 4 − 1/p, which is p = 2 + √3 ≈ 3.7321.

Counts for 500 waypoint unknowns: a full solve costs 250,000 operations after a 41,666,500-operation factorization. The banded solve costs 1,498 after a 998-operation factorization. That is 1.5% of a 1 ms tick at the speed assumed in Section 4.6.

Robotics linktrajectory planning

Re-planning inside a single tick

Planners edit waypoints all the time: an obstacle appears, a goal moves, a person steps into the aisle. Each edit changes the right-hand side only. With L and U in hand, a new path costs a few operations per waypoint, so the robot can re-plan between two motor commands.

The unknowns are useful in themselves. Mi is the acceleration the path demands at waypoint i, in metres per second squared, so comparing it with what the motors can deliver is a free safety check. For the default path in the lab the largest is 5.09 m/s². A real planner would also slow the timing down where that number is too high. This lab keeps one second per gap to keep the matrix simple.

4.8

Permutations: when rows must be exchanged

The last few minutes of the lecture, and the bridge to Lecture 5.

Everything so far assumed that no rows had to be exchanged. When a zero lands in a pivot position, elimination has to swap two rows before it can continue. Lecture 2 did that with a permutation matrix: the identity matrix with its rows put in a different order. Multiplying by it puts the rows of any matrix into that same order.

Strang closes the lecture by listing every permutation matrix of size 3. There are six.

All six 3 by 3 permutation matrices

The identity, which exchanges nothing. Three matrices that exchange two rows. Two that move all three rows round in a cycle.

Three facts about this family, all from the lecture:

  • Multiply any two and the result is in the list. Re-ordering twice is still a re-ordering.
  • Invert any one and the result is in the list. Putting the rows back is a re-ordering too. A family of matrices that is closed under multiplying and inverting in this way is called a group.
  • The inverse is the transpose: P−1 = PT. Each row of P has a single 1. Multiply a row by itself and you get 1. Multiply it by a different row and the ones miss each other, giving 0. That is the statement P PT = I.

How many are there? For size 3, the first row can go to any of 3 places, the second to either of the 2 left, and the last has 1 choice: 3 × 2 × 1 = 6. For size 4 the count is 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. For size n it is n × (n − 1) × … × 1, written n! and read "n factorial".

Lecture 5 puts these matrices to work. When exchanges are needed, the factorization becomes PA = LU.

Robotics linkwiring and numbering

Plugging three motors into three outputs is a permutation

A controller board has three outputs and the robot has three motors. There are six ways to plug them together, one for each matrix above. If a technician crosses the cables, the commands arrive re-ordered: the motors receive Pc where the software meant c.

The repair needs no rewiring. Send PTc, and the cables turn it into P PTc = c. With the commands (10, 20, 30) and the cycle wiring, the motors receive (20, 30, 10). Send (30, 10, 20) and they receive (10, 20, 30). The same trick renumbers joints between the order of a robot's hardware and the order its mathematical model uses.

First b. Forward with L: y1 = 3; y2 = 11 − 2·3 = 5; y3 = 16 − 1·3 − 2·5 = 3. Back with U: 3x3 = 3, so x3 = 1; 2x2 + 3 = 5, so x2 = 1; x1 + 1 + 1 = 3, so x1 = 1.

Second b. Forward: y1 = 1; y2 = 2 − 2 = 0; y3 = 1 − 1 − 0 = 0. Back: x3 = 0, x2 = 0, x1 = 1.

Answer: x = (1, 1, 1) and then x = (1, 0, 0). The second could have been seen at once: that b is the first column of A. No elimination was repeated for either.

P5A system has n = 30 unknowns. Using the exact counts (n³ − n)/3 and n², how many operations does it take to solve it for 100 different right-hand sides, from scratch each time and with one factorization? What happens to the two counts when n doubles to 60?cost

Factor: (27,000 − 30)/3 = 8,990. One right-hand side: 30² = 900.

From scratch: 100 × (8,990 + 900) = 989,000. Factor once: 8,990 + 100 × 900 = 98,990.

Answer: 989,000 against 98,990, which is 10 times less work. At n = 60 the factoring costs 71,980, eight times as much, and each right-hand side costs 3,600, four times as much.

Follow-up: however many right-hand sides there are, the saving at n = 30 can never pass (8,990 + 900)/900, about 11 times.

P7A rover starts at the origin facing along the x axis. Move A: drive 2 m forward. Move B: turn 90° to the left, then drive 1 m. Where is it? Then follow both undo orders and say where each one leaves it.robot

After A it is at (2, 0). After B it has turned to face up the y axis and driven to (2, 1).

Undo B, then A. Back up 1 m to (2, 0), turn 90° right to face along x again, back up 2 m to (0, 0). Home.

Undo A, then B. Back up 2 m, but the rover is still facing up the y axis, so it reaches (2, −1). Then undo B: back up 1 m to (2, −2) and turn 90° right.

Answer: the right order ends at (0, 0). The wrong order ends at (2, −2), which is 2.83 m from home, with the correct heading and the wrong position.

Follow-up: without the turn in B, both moves are drives along the x axis and either order ends at home.

P8A controller may spend 20,000 operations per tick on solving Ax = b, and the matrix is fixed. The system has n = 60. Does the solve fit if elimination is repeated every tick? Does it fit with stored factors? What is the largest n each way?robot

From scratch: (60³ − 60)/3 + 60² = 71,980 + 3,600 = 75,580. That is almost four ticks' worth, so it does not fit.

With L and U stored: 60² = 3,600, which is 18% of the budget. It fits easily.

Answer: only the factored solve fits. With stored factors the limit is n² ≤ 20,000, so n = 141. From scratch the limit is n = 38, where the cost is 19,722. At n = 39 it is 21,281.

Follow-up: the 71,980 operations of factoring still have to be done once, before the loop starts, when there is no deadline.

Q1What is (ABC)−1, and what everyday picture does Strang use for the rule?

C−1B−1A−1: the inverses in reverse order. Socks go on before shoes, so shoes come off before socks.

Q2In A = LU, what is on the diagonal of L, what is below it, and what is on the diagonal of U?

Ones on the diagonal of L. The multipliers below it, each in the position of the entry it cleared. The pivots on the diagonal of U. In A = LDU the pivots move into D and U gets ones as well.

Q3Elimination on a 3 by 3 matrix used the multipliers l21 = 3, l31 = 0, l32 = 2 and no row exchanges. Write L without any calculation.

Q4Why does E contain a number that no elimination step used, while L does not?

E applies the steps in the order they happen, so a later step acts on a row that an earlier step has already changed, and the multipliers get multiplied together. L rebuilds each row of A from finished rows of U, so each multiplier is used once, on its own.

Q5A matrix doubles in size. By what factor does the cost of factoring grow, and the cost of solving for one more right-hand side?

Factoring costs about n³/3, so it grows 8 times. Each right-hand side costs n², so it grows 4 times. The bigger the system, the more it pays to factor once.

Q6A colleague's control code computes A−1 once and multiplies it by b every tick. What would you change, and why?

Store L and U in place of the inverse and do the two triangular passes each tick. The inverse costs about three times as much to build, each tick costs the same n² either way, and the two passes are usually more accurate.

Q7In Lab 4.4, why does dragging a waypoint never cause a new factorization?

The spline matrix contains only fours and ones. It depends on how many waypoints there are and on nothing else. The waypoint positions appear only on the right-hand side, so a drag changes the right-hand side and the stored L and U still apply.
