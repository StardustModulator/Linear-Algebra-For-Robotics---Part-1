Column Space and Nullspace

Every matrix carries two subspaces, and they answer the two questions an engineer asks. The column space says what the matrix can produce. The nullspace says what it cannot see. For a robot arm they are "what the hand can do" and "how the joints can move while the hand stays still". The robot of this lecture is a three-link arm, which has one joint more than it needs.

6.0

Start here: one matrix, two questions

What this lecture is for, and the four ideas you need to bring with you.

Lecture 1 ended by asking whether Ax = b can be solved for every right-hand side b. This lecture builds the tool that answers it. Every matrix comes with two special sets of vectors. One holds every b the matrix can produce. The other holds every x the matrix wipes out to zero.

The two sets are called the column space and the nullspace. Both are subspaces, a word from the end of Lecture 5 that section 6.1 reviews. Here is everything else you need:

  • A vector is a list of numbers, such as (1, 2, 3, 4). Picture it as an arrow that starts at the origin, the point where every number is zero.
  • Rn means "all vectors with n numbers in them". R2 is a flat sheet of paper. R3 is the room you are sitting in. R4 has four numbers in each vector. Nobody can draw it, but the arithmetic is the same.
  • A linear combination of two vectors v and w is cv + dw: stretch each one by a number, then add the results.
  • Multiplying a matrix by a vector gives a linear combination of the matrix's columns. The entries of x say how much of each column to take. That was the column picture of Lecture 1.

The whole lecture runs on one matrix. It has four rows and three columns, so Ax = b is four equations in three unknowns.

The lecture's matrix
Ax = = x1+ x2+ x3

Three columns, each with four entries. Keep an eye on the third column. It is hiding something, and section 6.5 finds it.

Robotics linkwhy a robot cares

Every robot matrix raises the same two questions

A robot's matrix turns motor commands into motion, or positions into sensor readings. An engineer asks two things about it. What can come out? That set is the column space. What can go in and leave no trace on the output? That set is the nullspace.

The robot of this lecture is an arm with three joints that works in a flat plane. Two numbers are enough to say how its hand is moving, so the arm has one joint more than the job needs. Its column space is everything the hand can do. Its nullspace is every way the joints can move while the hand stays exactly where it is.

6.1

What makes a subspace

A short review of the idea Lecture 5 ended on.

A vector space is a set of vectors you cannot escape from by adding or stretching. Two rules must both hold.

  • Closed under addition. If v and w are in the set, then v + w is in the set.
  • Closed under multiplication by a number. If v is in the set, then cv is in the set for every number c.

The two rules fold into one test. Take any v and w from the set and any two numbers c and d. The combination cv + dw must still be in the set. In short: every linear combination stays inside.

A subspace is a vector space that sits inside a bigger one. Strang works inside R3, where the subspaces are easy to list:

  • all of R3;
  • any plane through the origin, which the lecture calls P;
  • any line through the origin, which the lecture calls L;
  • the zero vector on its own.

Why "through the origin" every time? Put c = 0 and d = 0 in the test. The combination is the zero vector, so the zero vector has to be in the set. A plane that misses the origin fails before you have tried anything else.

Trying the test on a plane and a line

The lecture draws P and L without numbers. This page picks a definite pair so that everything can be checked: P is every vector of the form (x, y, 0), and L is every multiple of (1, 1, 1).

3 (2, −1, 0) − 2 (−1, 3, 0) = (8, −9, 0)   3 (1, 1, 1) − 5 (1, 1, 1) = (−2, −2, −2)

The first result still ends in 0, so it is still in P. The second is still a multiple of (1, 1, 1), so it is still in L. No choice of numbers can break out of either set.

Robotics linksets of motions

The velocities a robot can produce form a subspace

Suppose a robot can move with velocity v and can also move with velocity w. Run the motors for the first at c times their speed and the motors for the second at d times theirs, and the robot moves with velocity cv + dw. Switch everything off and you get the zero vector. So the set of velocities open to a robot at one moment passes the test. It is a subspace.

One honest limit: a subspace has no edges, and a real motor has a top speed. The subspace records which directions of motion exist. How far along them the robot can go is a separate question.

6.2

Union and intersection

Two ways to make one set out of a plane and a line. Only one of them gives a subspace.

Take the plane P and the line L. There are two natural ways to combine them into a single set.

  • The union, written P ∪ L: every vector that is in P, or in L, or in both.
  • The intersection, written P ∩ L: only the vectors that are in both at once.

Strang asks the same question of each. Is it a subspace?

The union fails

Pick a vector v from the plane and a vector w from the line. Both belong to the union. Now add them. The sum usually lies in neither the plane nor the line, so it has left the union. The first rule is broken, and one broken rule is enough.

One sum that escapes
+ =

The third entry of the sum is 1, so it is off the plane. Its three entries are not equal, so it is off the line.

The intersection passes, for any two subspaces

Call the two subspaces S and T. Suppose v and w are both in S ∩ T. Then:

  • v + w is in S, because v and w are in S and S is a subspace;
  • v + w is in T, for the same reason;
  • so v + w is in both, which is what being in S ∩ T means.

Swap v + w for cv and the same three lines prove the second rule. The argument never asked what S and T look like. That is why it holds for every pair of subspaces, in any number of dimensions.

For a plane and a line the intersection is usually tiny: the zero vector alone. If the line happens to lie inside the plane, the intersection is the whole line. Either way it is a subspace.

Robotics linktwo machines

The motions two machines share, and the motions they do not

A table-top stage can slide a part along x and along y. A wall-mounted gantry can slide it along x and along z. Each machine's set of motions is a plane through the origin, so each is a subspace.

Intersection: the motions both can make. Only sliding along x, which is every multiple of (1, 0, 0). A line through the origin, and a subspace. If a job has to be handed from one machine to the other in mid-move, this is the set of moves that survive the handover.

Union: the motions at least one of them can make. Take (0, 1, 0) from the table and (0, 0, 1) from the gantry. Their sum is (0, 1, 1), and neither machine can do that. You cannot add a motion of one robot to a motion of another and expect either robot to perform the sum.

Watch out

Two subspaces always share at least the zero vector, so their intersection is never empty. And a union of two subspaces is a subspace only in the dull case where one of them already contains the other.

6.3

The column space

Everything a matrix can produce.

Now the first of the two subspaces that belong to a matrix. Take the columns of A. Form every linear combination of them. The set of all the results is the column space of A, written C(A). People also say that the columns span the column space.

The column space of the lecture's matrix
C(A) = every vector of the form x1+ x2+ x3

Here x1, x2 and x3 run over all numbers. Each choice gives one vector of the column space.

It is a subspace, and the reason is built into the definition. Add two mixes of the columns and you get another mix of the columns. Stretch a mix and you get another mix. Nothing can leave.

Where does it live? Each column has four entries, so each column is a vector in R4. The column space is therefore a subspace of R4.

How much of R4 does it cover? There are only three columns. Three arrows cannot fill a four-dimensional space, in the same way that two arrows cannot fill the room you are sitting in. So C(A) is some smaller subspace inside R4. Section 6.5 finds out exactly how small.

Robotics linkwhat the hand can do

C(J) is every hand velocity the arm can produce right now

The three-link arm has a matrix called the Jacobian, written J. It has two rows and three columns. Column 1 is the velocity of the hand when only the shoulder turns, at one radian per second. Column 2 is the same for the elbow, and column 3 for the wrist. Joint speeds go in, a hand velocity comes out:

=q̇1+q̇2+q̇3

So C(J) is the set of all hand velocities the arm can make in its present pose. Usually that is all of R2: any direction, any speed. Stretch the arm out straight and it shrinks to a single line, because then the hand can only move sideways. Lab 6.3 shows both cases.

6.4

Which b can be reached?

The question Lecture 1 ended on, answered in one sentence.

Does Ax = b have a solution for every b? For this matrix, no. There are four equations and only three unknowns. Three columns cannot fill four-dimensional space, so most right-hand sides are out of reach.

Which right-hand sides do allow a solution? Read the equation from left to right. The left side, Ax, is a combination of the columns of A. So the equation says "some combination of the columns equals b". That is true exactly when b is in the column space.

Beyond these, pick any x you like and compute Ax. Whatever comes out is a b that can be reached, because you have just reached it.

Elimination, the method of Lecture 2, turns the last two equations into 0 = 1 and 0 = 2. No x can satisfy those, so (1, 0, 0, 0) is outside the column space. For a general b the same two rows read 0 = b1 − 2b2 + b3 and 0 = 2b1 − 3b2 + b4. The vector b is in C(A) exactly when both of those numbers are zero. Lab 6.2 runs this test for any b you type.

Key idea

Ax = b can be solved exactly when b is in the column space of A. "Solvable" and "in the column space" are the same statement.

Robotics linkmore sensors than unknowns

Four readings for three unknowns: the readings have to agree

The lecture's matrix is taller than it is wide. In a robot that shape appears whenever there are more sensor readings than quantities to work out. Here is one case. A robot stands in a room 6 m by 4 m, square to the walls, with one range sensor facing each wall. Every sensor sits the same unknown distance d out from the robot's centre. The unknowns are x, y and d: three numbers. The readings are four numbers.

=

An exact answer exists only if b lies in the column space of S. For this matrix that comes down to one condition, b1 + b2 = b3 + b4: the east-west pair of sensors and the north-south pair must tell the same story about d. Perfect readings of 2.3, 3.3, 1.3 and 2.3 m pass, and give x = 2.5, y = 1.5, d = 0.2. Let the north sensor read 5 cm long and the condition fails. The readings now contradict each other and no exact solution exists.

Real sensors are always a little off, so real readings almost never land in the column space. Least squares, much later in the course, deals with that by finding the nearest b that does.

For the arm, the same sentence reads: a commanded hand velocity can be produced exactly when it lies in C(J).

6.5

Independent columns and pivot columns

Does every column pull its weight?

Three columns went into C(A). Did all three contribute something? A set of columns is independent when none of them is a combination of the others, so each one brings a direction the others cannot make. The lecture's columns fail that test:

The hidden relation
+ =

Column 3 is already a combination of columns 1 and 2. Anything you can build with all three, you can build with the first two alone.

So column 3 adds nothing new. Throw it away and the column space does not shrink at all. Strang keeps columns 1 and 2 and calls them the pivot columns: reading from left to right, they are the ones that bring a new direction. He points out that the choice is a convention. Columns 1 and 3 would do equally well, and so would columns 2 and 3. Any two of these three columns produce the same plane.

The name comes from elimination, which finds the pivot columns automatically. Lab 6.2 circles the two pivots in red, and Lecture 7 turns the idea into a method.

That settles the size of the column space. It is every combination of two independent vectors, (1, 2, 3, 4) and (1, 1, 1, 1). Two independent directions make a plane. The number of independent directions is called the dimension, so C(A) is a two-dimensional subspace of R4: a flat plane through the origin, sitting inside four-dimensional space.

Robotics linkdoes every joint help?

A dependent column is a joint that gives the hand nothing new

Take the three-link arm with its joints at 70°, −60° and −70° (section 6.8 works out its Jacobian). In that pose

column 3 = −0.40 × column 1 + 0.81 × column 2

Whatever hand velocity the wrist can make, the shoulder and elbow could have made together. For the job of moving the hand, joint 3 gives nothing that joints 1 and 2 could not already give.

This is no accident of the pose. The three columns of J are arrows in a flat plane, and a flat plane has room for only two independent directions. A matrix with two rows and three columns always has a dependent column. The spare joint is far from wasted, though. The next section shows what it buys.

6.6

The nullspace

Everything a matrix sends to zero.

The second subspace asks the opposite question. Forget b. Which vectors x does the matrix turn into the zero vector? The set of all solutions of Ax = 0 is the nullspace of A, written N(A).

Where does it live? The vector x has one entry for each column of A, which makes three. So the nullspace is a set of vectors in R3. The column space was in R4. The two subspaces of one matrix live in different spaces, and it is the first thing to get straight. For a matrix with m rows and n columns, C(A) is inside Rm and N(A) is inside Rn.

Finding the nullspace of the lecture's matrix
= + − =

The zero vector (0, 0, 0) always solves Ax = 0. Is there anything else? Yes, and section 6.5 already found it. Column 1 plus column 2 minus column 3 is zero, so x = (1, 1, −1) is a solution. So is (2, 2, −2), and (−5, −5, 5), and every multiple c (1, 1, −1).

The nullspace of this matrix is every multiple of (1, 1, −1). That is a line through the origin in R3.

Notice that nothing new had to be computed. "Column 3 equals column 1 plus column 2" and "(1, 1, −1) is in the nullspace" are one fact written twice. A relation between the columns is a non-zero vector in the nullspace. If the columns were independent, the nullspace would hold the zero vector and nothing more.

Robotics linkself-motion

N(J) is every joint motion the hand cannot feel

For the arm, J q̇ = 0 asks for joint speeds that add up to zero hand velocity. The joints turn, the elbow swings, and the hand stays exactly where it is. Roboticists call this self-motion.

A three-joint arm doing a two-dimensional job always has at least a line of self-motions, because its Jacobian always has a dependent column. That is what the word redundant means in robotics: more joints than the task needs.

You own a redundant arm. Put a fingertip on the table and keep it there. Your elbow is still free to swing up and down. That swing is a walk along a nullspace.

6.7

Why the nullspace is always a subspace

A two-line proof that works for every matrix.

The name "nullspace" promises a subspace, so the two rules have to be checked. Suppose v and w are both in the nullspace. That means Av = 0 and Aw = 0.

The proof
A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0 = 0    A(cv) = c (Av) = c 0 = 0

The first line says the sum v + w is in the nullspace. The second says every multiple cv is in the nullspace. Both rules hold, so the nullspace is a subspace.

With the lecture's numbers: v = (2, 2, −2) and w = (−5, −5, 5) are both in N(A). Their sum is (−3, −3, 3), which is −3 (1, 1, −1), and A sends it to (0, 0, 0, 0).

Each line leans on one property of matrix multiplication. A matrix times a sum is the sum of the products, and a matrix times a stretched vector is the stretched product. Those two properties are what the word "linear" in linear algebra refers to.

Robotics linkmixing self-motions

Self-motions can be scaled, reversed and blended

Because N(J) is a subspace, a self-motion run at twice the speed is still a self-motion. Run it backwards, which is c = −1, and it is still a self-motion. Add two self-motions together and the hand still does not move.

The practical consequence is large. A controller can add any amount of self-motion on top of any joint command, and the hand will not notice. That is how a redundant arm steers its elbow away from an obstacle while its hand carries on with the job.

6.8

The robot: what the hand can do, and the motion it cannot feel

One three-link arm, one Jacobian, both subspaces at once.

Now put both subspaces to work on one machine. The arm has three links, 1.6 m, 1.3 m and 1.0 m long, joined by a shoulder, an elbow and a wrist. It moves in a flat plane, and we care only about where the hand is. That takes two numbers, so the hand's velocity is a vector in R2. One simplification to keep in mind: a real arm works in three dimensions and usually has to aim its hand as well as place it.

Where the Jacobian comes from

Turn one joint a little and hold the others still. Everything beyond that joint swings round it like a door on its hinge. The hand moves at right angles to the line from that joint to the hand, and it moves faster the longer that line is. So column i of J is the arrow from joint i to the hand, turned through a quarter of a circle. Three joints give three columns. A hand velocity has two components, so there are two rows.

One pose, worked out

Joint angles 70°, −60° and −70° put the hand at (2.327, 0.863). Each angle is measured from the link before it.

(−0.863)(−0.299)+(0.640)(0.600)+(0.866)(−0.742)=0.258 + 0.384 − 0.642=0

Counting directions

The number of independent columns of a matrix is called its rank. Lecture 7 makes rank a central idea. For now it is a head count of the columns that matter.

  • In this pose the rank is 2. Two independent arrows in a plane reach everywhere in it, so C(J) is all of R2 and has dimension 2. The hand can move in any direction.
  • There are three joints. Two directions of joint space are used up moving the hand, which leaves 3 − 2 = 1. So N(J) has dimension 1: a line through the origin of joint-speed space. Every multiple of (−0.299, 0.600, −0.742) moves the joints and leaves the hand alone.

Robotics linkwhy pay for a spare joint

The spare joint is what lets a robot work around things

An arm with exactly as many joints as its task needs has very little choice of posture once the hand is placed. A redundant arm has a whole family of postures for the same hand position, and self-motion is the road between them.

It can reach round a pillar. It can back a joint away from the end of its travel. It can hold a tool tip perfectly still while it moves its elbow out of a person's way. In each case the task sees nothing, because the extra motion lies in the nullspace.

Watch out

Stretch the arm out straight, or fold it flat, and all three columns become parallel. The column space collapses from a plane to a line and the nullspace grows from a line to a plane. The hand loses a direction at the very moment the joints gain a useless one. Robot people call that pose a singularity.

A nullspace vector describes joint speeds at one instant. To keep the hand still over a long move, the robot has to recompute the nullspace as the pose changes. Lab 6.3 does that on every frame.

6.9

The solutions of Ax = b are not a subspace

Change the right-hand side from zero to anything else and the subspace is gone.

The solutions of Ax = 0 form a subspace. It is tempting to guess that the solutions of Ax = b do too, for any b. They do not, and one check is enough. Every subspace contains the zero vector. But A times the zero vector is the zero vector, which is not b. So when b is not zero, the zero vector is not a solution, and the set of solutions cannot be a subspace.

The lecture's example, b = (1, 2, 3, 4)
A= A= A=

Two solutions are (1, 0, 0) and (0, −1, 1). The zero vector is not one. Adding fails as well: the sum of the two solutions is (1, −1, 1), and A sends that to (2, 4, 6, 8), which is 2b and not b.

So what shape do the solutions make? Strang's answer: a line that does not pass through the origin. In bigger problems it can be a plane that misses the origin. Flat, like a subspace, but in the wrong place.

A look ahead

The two solutions above differ by (1, 0, 0) − (0, −1, 1) = (1, 1, −1), and that is the nullspace vector. It is no coincidence. Every solution of Ax = b is one particular solution plus something from the nullspace. Here the full set is (1, 0, 0) + c (1, 1, −1): the nullspace line, picked up and shifted. This lecture only hints at the pattern. A later lecture of the course develops it properly.

Robotics linkchoosing joint speeds

One wanted hand velocity, a whole line of ways to get it

Ask the arm of section 6.8 for a hand velocity of (0.2, 0.1) m/s. The equation J q̇ = v has more unknowns than equations, and it has infinitely many solutions: one particular solution plus any amount of self-motion. Three of them, in rad/s:

  • (−0.055, 0.099, 0.103), the shortest of all, with length 0.153;
  • (−0.096, 0.182, 0), which holds the wrist still, with length 0.206;
  • (−0.145, 0.279, −0.120), the shortest one plus 0.3 of self-motion, with length 0.337.

All three give exactly the same hand velocity. In joint-speed space they lie on one straight line, parallel to N(J) and missing the origin. That line is not a subspace, for the reason this section gave.

The robot gets to choose a point on the line. Most controllers start from the shortest solution, which keeps the joints as slow as possible, and then add a deliberate dose of self-motion when there is something to avoid.

6.10

Two ways to describe a subspace

Build it up from vectors, or cut it down with equations.

Strang closes by pointing out that his two subspaces were made in opposite ways.

  • Build it up. Start with a few vectors and take all their combinations. That is how the column space was made. You are handed some building blocks and told: everything these can make.
  • Cut it down. Start with the whole space and keep only the vectors that satisfy some equations. That is how the nullspace was made. Each row of Ax = 0 is one condition, and only the vectors that pass every condition are kept.

Any subspace can be described either way. Which description you are given depends on where the subspace came from, and a good deal of linear algebra is the work of turning one description into the other.

The same line, both ways
built up: every multiple of (1, 1, −1)   cut down: every x with x1 + x3 = 0 and x2 + x3 = 0

Both describe the nullspace of the lecture's matrix. The first names one vector that generates the line. The second gives two equations that the line satisfies. They are what is left of the four equations Ax = 0 once elimination has removed the repeats.

Robotics linkactuators and constraints

A rover's allowed velocities, described from both sides

A differential-drive rover has two driven wheels side by side, of radius r, a distance W apart. Its body velocity is three numbers: forward speed vx, sideways speed vy and turning rate ω.

By generators: the allowed velocities are everything the two wheels can produce, the column space C(G). By constraints: they are everything that obeys "no sideways slip", the nullspace of the one-row matrix [0 1 0]. Both describe the same plane, vy = 0, inside the three-dimensional space of body velocities.

With r = 0.1 m and W = 0.5 m, a wanted velocity of (0.3, 0, 0.4) is in the plane, and wheel speeds of 2 and 4 rad/s produce it. A wanted velocity of (0.3, 0.1, 0) fails both descriptions at once: no wheel speeds produce it, and it breaks the constraint. This assumes ideal wheels that never skid.

P1Which of these sets of vectors (x, y) are subspaces of R2? (a) All multiples of (1, 2). (b) All vectors with x + y = 1. (c) All vectors with x ≥ 0.subspace test

(a) Add two multiples of (1, 2) and you get a multiple of (1, 2). Stretch one and you get another. For instance 3 (1, 2) − 4 (2, 4) = (−5, −10), which is −5 (1, 2). A line through the origin: a subspace.

(b) The zero vector has x + y = 0, so it is missing. Adding fails too: (1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1) has x + y = 2. A line that misses the origin.

(c) (1, 0) is in the set, but −1 times it is (−1, 0), which is out. Sums stay inside, so only the second rule fails. One failure is enough.

Answer: only (a) is a subspace.

P2In R3, let S be the plane of all vectors (0, y, z) and T the plane of all vectors (x, 0, z). Describe S ∩ T. Then find one sum that shows S ∪ T is not a subspace.union, intersection

A vector in both planes needs its first entry to be 0 (to be in S) and its second entry to be 0 (to be in T). That leaves (0, 0, z).

For the union, take (0, 1, 0) from S and (1, 0, 0) from T. Their sum is (1, 1, 0). Its first entry is not 0, so it is not in S. Its second entry is not 0, so it is not in T.

Answer: S ∩ T is the z axis, every multiple of (0, 0, 1): a line through the origin, and a subspace. The union is not closed under addition.

P3Describe the column space of . Which space is it inside? Is (2, 4, 6) in it? Is (1, 2, 4)?column space

The columns have three entries, so the column space is inside R3. The second column is twice the first, so it adds nothing. Every combination is a multiple of (1, 2, 3).

(2, 4, 6) = 2 (1, 2, 3): yes, it is in the column space. For (1, 2, 4) the first entry would need a multiplier of 1, and then the third entry would be 3 and not 4: no.

Answer: C(A) is the line through the origin of R3 in the direction (1, 2, 3). It has one pivot column and dimension 1. (2, 4, 6) is in it and (1, 2, 4) is not.

P4For , find the condition on b = (b1, b2, b3) that makes Ax = b solvable. Then solve it for b = (2, 3, 5).which b

Write the three equations: x1 = b1, x2 = b2 and x1 + x2 = b3. The first two fix x completely. The third then has no freedom left: it holds only if b3 = b1 + b2.

For (2, 3, 5): 2 + 3 = 5, so the condition holds, and x = (2, 3).

Answer: solvable exactly when b3 = b1 + b2. That one equation describes the column space, a plane inside R3. For (2, 3, 5) the solution is x = (2, 3).

Follow-up: change the 5 to a 6. Now 2 + 3 ≠ 6, the vector has left the column space, and there is no solution.

P5Find the nullspace of . Which space is it inside, and what shape is it?nullspace

A vector x has three entries, so the nullspace is inside R3. The second row is twice the first, so the two equations are really one: x1 + 2x2 + 3x3 = 0.

One equation in three unknowns leaves two unknowns free. Choose x2 = 1, x3 = 0 and you need x1 = −2. Choose x2 = 0, x3 = 1 and you need x1 = −3.

Answer: the nullspace is every combination of (−2, 1, 0) and (−3, 0, 1): a plane through the origin of R3, of dimension 2. Each of the two vectors records one relation between the columns: column 2 is twice column 1, and column 3 is three times column 1.

P6For the lecture's matrix and b = (2, 3, 4, 5), find two different solutions by looking at the columns. Then write down every solution.shifted nullspace

b is column 3 itself, so x = (0, 0, 1) works. Column 3 is also column 1 plus column 2, so x = (1, 1, 0) works as well.

The two differ by (1, 1, 0) − (0, 0, 1) = (1, 1, −1), which is the nullspace vector from section 6.6.

Answer: every solution is (0, 0, 1) + c (1, 1, −1). It is a line parallel to the nullspace that misses the origin, so it is not a subspace. Check: the sum of the two solutions, (1, 1, 1), gives Ax = (4, 6, 8, 10), which is 2b.

P7A planar arm has three links of length 1. The shoulder is at 0°, the elbow is bent 90° and the wrist is bent 90°, so the joints sit at (0, 0), (1, 0) and (1, 1) and the hand at (0, 1). Find the Jacobian, C(J) and N(J).robot

Each column is the arrow from that joint to the hand, turned a quarter turn anticlockwise. Turning (a, b) gives (−b, a).

  • Shoulder at (0, 0): arrow (0, 1), turned (−1, 0).
  • Elbow at (1, 0): arrow (−1, 1), turned (−1, −1).
  • Wrist at (1, 1): arrow (−1, 0), turned (0, −1).
J=

Columns 1 and 3 point along different axes, so they are independent and the rank is 2. For the nullspace solve −q̇1 − q̇2 = 0 and −q̇2 − q̇3 = 0. Set q̇3 = 1: then q̇2 = −1 and q̇1 = 1.

Answer: C(J) is all of R2, so the hand can move in any direction. N(J) is every multiple of (1, −1, 1): shoulder and wrist turn one way, the elbow turns the other way at the same speed, and the hand stays still.

P8The arm of P7 is now stretched straight along the x axis, with the hand at (3, 0). Find J. Can the hand move in the direction (1, 0)? What are the dimensions of C(J) and N(J)?robot

The arrows from the joints to the hand are (3, 0), (2, 0) and (1, 0). Turned a quarter turn they are (0, 3), (0, 2) and (0, 1).

J=

All three columns are multiples of (0, 1), so the rank is 1. The top row is all zeros: no joint speeds can give the hand any velocity along x. The nullspace is every solution of the one remaining equation, 3q̇1 + 2q̇2 + q̇3 = 0.

Answer: the hand cannot move along (1, 0). C(J) is the line of multiples of (0, 1), dimension 1. N(J) is a plane, dimension 2, spanned for example by (−2, 3, 0) and (−1, 0, 3). The dimensions add to 1 + 2 = 3, the number of joints, exactly as they did in P7 with 2 + 1.

P9Back to the bent arm of P7. The hand must move with velocity (1, 0). Find the joint speeds that do it with the wrist held still. Then the ones that do it with the shoulder held still. Then the shortest set of all.robot

Wrist still, q̇3 = 0: the second row gives q̇2 = 0, and the first gives −q̇1 = 1. So q̇ = (−1, 0, 0).

All solutions: add any multiple of the self-motion from P7: (−1, 0, 0) + c (1, −1, 1) = (c − 1, −c, c).

Shoulder still: c = 1 gives (0, −1, 1).

Shortest: the squared length is (c − 1)² + c² + c² = 3c² − 2c + 1, smallest at c = 1/3. That gives (−2/3, −1/3, 1/3), of length about 0.816.

Answer: (−1, 0, 0), then (0, −1, 1), then (−2/3, −1/3, 1/3). All three give the hand velocity (1, 0). They lie on one line in joint-speed space, parallel to N(J) and missing the origin.

P10A cart runs on a rail between two walls 10 m apart. Its position is x. A rear sensor reads x + d and a front sensor reads (10 − x) + d, where d is an unknown offset shared by both. A tape along the rail reads x directly. (a) The three readings are 5, 7 and 4. Find x and d. (b) Next day they are 5, 7 and 5. What should the robot conclude?robot

Move the known 10 to the right-hand side. There are three equations and two unknowns:

=

Subtract the second equation from the first: 2x = b1 − b2. The third says x = b3. Both can be true only if b1 − b2 = 2b3. That is the condition for b to be in the column space.

(a) b = (5, −3, 4): 5 − (−3) = 8 = 2 · 4. Consistent. Then x = 4 and d = 5 − 4 = 1.

(b) b = (5, −3, 5): 5 − (−3) = 8 but 2 · 5 = 10. The readings contradict each other.

Answer: (a) x = 4 m and d = 1 m. (b) There is no exact solution, because b has left the column space. At least one sensor is wrong, and these three numbers alone cannot say which. An extra sensor never guarantees a better answer, but it does give the robot a way to notice that something is off.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each one.

Q1A plane and a line both pass through the origin of R3, and the line is not in the plane. Is their union a subspace? Is their intersection?

The union is not. Add a non-zero vector of the plane to a non-zero vector of the line and the sum is in neither, so the union is not closed under addition. The intersection is the zero vector alone, and that is a subspace, the smallest one.

Q2The lecture's matrix has 4 rows and 3 columns. In which space does its column space live? In which space does its nullspace live?

The columns have four entries, so C(A) is a subspace of R4. A vector x has one entry per column, three in all, so N(A) is a subspace of R3.

Q3Without elimination, decide whether b = (3, 4, 5, 6) is in the column space of the lecture's matrix.

It is. Column 1 plus twice column 2 is (1, 2, 3, 4) + (2, 2, 2, 2) = (3, 4, 5, 6). So x = (1, 2, 0) solves Ax = b. The check from section 6.4 agrees: 3 − 8 + 5 = 0 and 6 − 12 + 6 = 0.

Q4Column 3 of the lecture's matrix equals column 1 plus column 2. What does that one fact tell you about the column space, and what does it tell you about the nullspace?

For the column space: column 3 adds nothing, so C(A) is built from columns 1 and 2 alone and is a two-dimensional plane inside R4. For the nullspace: column 1 plus column 2 minus column 3 is zero, so (1, 1, −1) and all its multiples are in N(A), a line in R3.

Q5Why can the solutions of Ax = b never form a subspace when b is not zero?

A subspace must contain the zero vector, and A times the zero vector is zero, which is not b. The solutions form a flat set that misses the origin: the nullspace shifted by one particular solution.

Q6A planar arm with three joints only has to place its hand. In an ordinary pose, what are the dimensions of C(J) and N(J), and what does a vector in N(J) do?

C(J) has dimension 2: the hand can move in every direction of the plane. N(J) has dimension 3 − 2 = 1. A vector in it is a set of three joint speeds whose effects on the hand cancel. The elbow and wrist swing and the hand stays put. That is self-motion.

Q7The same arm is stretched out perfectly straight. What happens to the two subspaces?

All three columns of J now point the same way, across the arm. The rank drops to 1. C(J) shrinks to a line, so the hand can no longer move along the arm. N(J) grows to a plane of dimension 3 − 1 = 2. The two dimensions still add up to the number of joints.

For your article

One-line summary: the column space is everything a matrix can produce, the nullspace is everything it cannot see, and both are subspaces.

A robot sentence you can reuse: the column space of the Jacobian is what the hand can do, and the nullspace is how the joints can move while the hand stays still.
